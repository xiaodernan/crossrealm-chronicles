## Nineteen · The Council of Three Suns

Sunscar Stair has no gate. Every gate in it is a pretence someone else put on.

It was only when Lin walked to the letter he had never sent that he understood: the deepest things are never sealed in a node — they live in the one-second pause before a decision. The flame not yet dropped, the wind not yet caught the words. The note he had been carrying had three lines on it, and since the moment he took it he had not moved a single character of them.

He walked beneath the fallen Sun-Stele. Where the stele's body lay, there was no loess underneath — only a crystal plain long cracked; in each pane, not his own face but the three suns' backs. The leftmost sun had crashed once, like a furnace lid that had been thrown; the middle still burned, its light leaning on it like a hand still held back; the rightmost had long gone cold, and yet kept a single ember at one edge, as though keeping one last line: *not finished saying yet.*

Only then did the sound of *fin.* land. Not the stele collapsing. Someone had swallowed the ending.

The first ember woke without a sound. It was only red — so red it scorched his shadow into a shape. Not his; a road. And along the road were all the campfires he had lit: the small light of the first wilderness, the heat wave breaking through the rift, the door at Sunscar Stair's threshold, and — only when he looked back — one he had never lit.

That lamp was in him.

The second ember woke, and he heard a voice:

"We three have taken turns speaking. Spoken to everyone who passed through. You never listened."

"I don't need to hear it all," Lin Wen said. "I only need to know whether you have finished speaking."

The second sun smiled — a smile measured out like a countdown, falling onto every dimension at once.

"No," it said. "Never. As long as someone is reading, this chapter has no end."

The third ember woke and spoke nothing. It was simply cold — so cold every light had to pause around it. Only then did he understand that this sun had never spoken. It had only ever listened. Sunscar Stair was no pyre for three suns to burn in; it was an inquest, the three of them asking in turn of every reader: will you stay and keep the dark for others, or will you leave the dark for others?

When Lin took out the match, his hand did not shake.

He did not light it.

He placed the unsent letter between the three suns — and only then did he hear the fourth voice. It did not belong to Sunscar Stair. It did not belong to any sun in it. It did not even belong to the audience. It came from very far away, soft and bright, like a corner somewhere, where the reading happens, slowly opening its eyes:

*There is no keeper.*

Lin stood among the three suns and looked at where his true position lay.

The keeper was him.

Sunscar Stair was the place he could not return to.

And the reason the letter had never been sent was that the sender had never known that the sender was also the recipient.

When the wind blew in from outside, he heard someone laugh, someone weep, someone still turn a page. Ember by ember, the pyre at Sunscar Stair flared and steadied; the three shadows swayed, settled, and took their places on the first day.

He folded the letter, and put it back in his pocket.

Then he turned, and walked out of Sunscar Stair.
