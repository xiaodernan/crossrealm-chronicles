## One Hundred and Eleven: The Characters

Thirty days after the autumn equinox, the first rain of the season fell on the courtyard — earlier than any autumn rain in seven hundred years.

The old man came again.

This time he carried no basket. No bundle. No letters. He walked in wearing a freshly laundered white hemp robe and set his shoes at the doorway as if he had been born there.

His mother asked, "Did you not say you would not sleep that night either?"

"The moon will be full tonight," the old man said. "The fifteenth."

"The fifteenth — the fire watch," A-Shi said.

"The fifteenth — the fire watch," the old man agreed.

Xiao Huo walked out from beside the stove and sat himself on the threshold.

The old man came and knelt in front of him. He drew from his robe a small piece of black charcoal — the sort Xiao Huo had seen before. Black, light, with a faint smoky halo. It was a chip of coal from the ancestor-fire itself; the very ember the first keepers had carried seven hundred years before.

"You will learn three characters from me," the old man said. "火. 灶. 守."

Xiao Huo nodded.

The old man took the charcoal and drew one stroke with it on the ground before the stove — 火, the fire character: six strokes, an anchor of a stroke in the center, a forked head, a single sweep of the ground line.

"This character," he said, "has been alive seven hundred years."

Xiao Huo stared at it.

He pointed at the stove with one finger. "That one?"

The old man shook his head.

"First learn fire," he said. "The character for stove has one more stroke. The character for keeper has yet another."

He drew 灶 below 火, and 守 below 灶 — three characters, one above the other.

"火 — 灶 — 守," he repeated, slowly, once.

Xiao Huo watched him for a long while.

Then he took the charcoal in his small fingers and started copying the strokes on the ground. He made one attempt, rubbed it out, made another, rubbed it out again. Thirteen times he tried. Only on the thirteenth did his characters begin to settle.

No one in the house made any sound.

His mother appeared at the kitchen doorway, looked at the three characters in the dust, and said:

"That one looks like fire."

A-Shi looked at them and said:

"That one looks like Xiao Huo."

The old man looked at the three characters a long time, then said nothing at all.

He asked Xiao Huo to do one more thing.

He asked him to carve the characters into the kitchen wall — the wall above the stove, at eye height for the fire, two feet from the stove iron. He sat as close as he could and checked every stroke Xiao Huo carved, one by one, until all three characters were standing steady.

火. 灶. 守.

The last stroke finished, the flame leapt once.

Everyone knew it.

Seven hundred years, and no one had ever taught anyone how to write the characters for the fire they were keeping. Tonight, someone had.

"Bank the stove tonight," the old man said.

"I only know how to cover it in ash," Xiao Huo said.

"That is enough," the old man said. "If you know how to bank the stove in ash, then you are a keeper."

The stove.

Seven hundred years.

No one had ever been taught. No one. Not one keeper. Not in seven hundred years.

Today.

There has been.
