## Seventy-one: The Painter

On the third day after the snow stopped, deep winter was still with them.

The stove burned fierce and steady, the whole iron body glowing a low, dark red. A-Shi wiped the kitchen cloth over the stove ledge, poured himself a bowl of plain rice porridge — and heard someone cough outside.

He opened the door. Wind blew in, carrying a very faint scent of pine soot and old paper.

A man stood at the threshold.

He wore a traveller's hat, a short grey-brown tunic, and a bulging cloth-wrapped pack over one shoulder — and in his other hand he held a small bamboo basket. His footprints behind him stretched unbroken all the way from the mountain foot into the very mouth of this stove's house.

"Good master," the traveller said, his voice low, gritted like sandpaper on wood, "may I borrow a night's fire?"

A-Shi ushered him in.

The traveller did not look at him at first. He walked straight to the stove and stared into the flames. He stared so long that A-Shi thought he must be seeing something else altogether. When he finally spoke, it was quiet.

"I am a painter. I walk through the world, and I paint fire."

A-Shi hesitated. Then he asked:

"Which fires?"

The traveller did not answer directly. He drew out a roll of paper from his pack, spread it on the low table by the stove, and unhooked a brush from his belt — the ink on its tip was still wet.

"I started at the beacon on the ridge," he said. "Three months of painting fire. The beacon, the night-lamp on the white stone, the ever-burning lamp at the crossroads, Uncle Ye's pipe coal, Li Aunty's incense pot — every place, I painted it."

He opened the roll. A-Shi saw: pages and pages of flames, every one a different shape. Red flames, orange flames, blue flames, ember-red flames, flames that glared white, flames that had collapsed into a single shadow. Some stretched upright, some lay flat, some curled like flower buds about to open.

"Every one of them looks like fire," A-Shi said.

The traveller shook his head.

"No. Each one looks like somebody else's fire. Not one of them looks like itself."

He drew out one blank sheet, spread it on the table, sat upright, and slowly dipped his brush. The stroke moved slowly, as if held back by something. One stroke. Then another. Then a third.

On the seventh stroke, the brush hitched — and everything changed.

A-Shi came close to look. It was a flame, drawn with only seven strokes. Every stroke was a direction he knew well — the ridge's seven beacon-lamps. They did not lie in a single direction — instead they pointed outward in every direction at once, like a flame, like a ridge line, like an open pair of hands.

"You — " A-Shi stammered.

The traveller lifted his head.

"Your family's fire," he said, "is not like any other. Three months I painted, and I could never finish it."

He lowered the brush.

"Now I understand why. This stove has been burning for six hundred years — the oldest fire in the world. And the oldest fire on earth is not something any man can pass off as his own painting."

A-Shi stared at the seven strokes.

"What is this?"

"Not fire," the traveller said. "This is what fire remembers."

He folded the painting and pressed it into A-Shi's hands.

"This one is yours. Six hundred years — somebody should paint this."

The paper was feather-light, like the first snowflake to fall six hundred years ago.

While the traveller tied his pack, A-Shi asked, almost to himself:

"Are you the one who came six hundred years ago? The one who borrowed the stove-fire from Grandfather and lit the ever-burning lamp at the crossroads?"

The traveller did not turn.

"I am only a painter," he said.

Then he was gone. A-Shi stood in the doorway and watched his footprints fade in the fresh snow until there was nothing left to see.

Dawn came. A-Shi framed the painting and hung it beside the stove.

Baiyin asked about it later. After a long silence, A-Shi answered:

"He is not the peddler from six hundred years ago. The peddler from six hundred years ago was a spark himself — he spent his whole life looking for a fire that burns six hundred years."

He looked at the stove.

"And he found it."
