# The Unchanted Chronicle of Crossrealm: The Keeper of the Anchor Fire

## Eighty-two: The Return

A-Shi walked home the way he came.

Seven full days — the first through the mist of the chimney ridge, the last across the northern ash-flat, one gradation of grey to the next, white to amber to rust. He passed three unfamiliar lamps, met two fire-keeping families he had never seen, and spent one night with a keeper older than any soul he had ever met.

On the final evening, he reached the village.

The stove was burning, as it always was. The fire-seed tree in the yard had grown a full foot taller; its needles were so red they looked like flames fixed against the evening sky. His mother was standing at the door.

"Back?" she said.

A-Shi nodded.

He set down the bundle on the kitchen floor and lifted the iron canister he had been guarding since the mountain — the one holding the coal that had fallen from the heart of the white stone, six hundred years old and never once burned.

He set it on the stove-edge. The stove flared once, gently.

"Where did you go, A-Shi?" his mother asked.

He looked at the fire for a long time before answering.

"North," he said. "Three lamps north. Same as our stove, six hundred years. I met Grandma Bai's sister."

His mother nodded.

"You are a keeper," she said. "Keepers are not brave — they are simply the ones who do not regret."

A-Shi laughed softly.

"It is true," he said. "Keepers never quite get to regret."

He set down the wooden plaque the other keeper had given him — `守火 · 守到全案` — on the kitchen table.

He took the canister from his arms and laid it before the hearth. The coal inside had not dimmed at all.

"Together," A-Shi said.

His mother looked at him for a long moment — and understood. A-Shi meant to merge the unlit coal with the stove: to complete, at last, the road six hundred years ago the first keeper had started when he lent a spark to a traveller heading south.

She fed one final log into the hearth.

The fire climbed, and climbed, and rose brighter than it ever had in daylight — and this time, it did not stop at the walls. Light burst through the roof, through the open sky, and the ever-burning lamp at the crossroads answered. The beacon on the white stone answered. Every light on the eastern ridge, every lamp in every kitchen of the valley — all at once.

A-Shi said nothing.

He stretched out one hand and touched the very centre of the flame. It did not burn him — it was warm, soft, almost weightless, as though some presence were cupping his fingers.

His mother straightened her tunic.

"You came home," she said.

A-Shi shook his head.

"I came home halfway," he said.

He turned to the fire.

"The stove never left," he said.

The stove leapt once more.

Two bowls of rice porridge were set before them at last. Every ember in the hearth glowed steady, brighter than it had on any night yet — warm enough to reach every corner of the house.

Just before dawn, his mother found a paper on the table — a single character written in a firm, unsteady young hand:

记.

Outside, the fire-seed tree stood straight and tall — and its needles burned red, six hundred years of red, all the way to the sky.
