## Ninety-eight: The Loft

A little before dawn, A-Shi heard something above him.

Very soft. Like a small step on the wooden loft floor — a creak that was not his mother, not Xiao Huo, not the wind.

He listened without moving.

Nothing.

He went up the ladder into the loft.

The trap door was open.

He had not opened it.

Inside, the small room was washed with thin, pale morning light. Nothing had been disturbed. The pile of old winter clothes in the corner had not been moved. The stack of yellowing paper in the far corner had not been shifted.

And in the middle of the room, on the old wobbly table nobody used anymore, sat a single coal.

Plain. Very ordinary. No larger than a knuckle. Still faintly glowing at its very center, as though something deep inside it had never gone out.

A-Shi walked to it slowly.

He did not touch it.

He looked at it for a long time.

It was the kind of coal his grandfather used to carry from the mountain on the day of the first frost — the kind of charcoal his great-grandfather used to set special nights apart for — the kind the family had always brought home from the white stone on the northern ridge.

He lifted it gently with one hand.

It was warm.

Not just warm from sitting in the loft — warm from within. The way the burned center of a long-since-extinguished fireplace stays warm hours after the last wood has ashed over.

There was a small piece of paper tucked under its edge.

He unfolded it with two fingers, very carefully.

On it, a single line:

The fire carries everything forward.

A-Shi stood in the loft for a long while.

Then he carried the coal down the ladder.

He approached the stove.

He opened the stove's iron door.

He placed the coal inside.

The stove did not explode. It did not roar. It simply — brightened.

The kitchen filled with a gentle white-gold light that felt less like a flame and more like warmth arriving from somewhere far away — from a stove so ancient nobody alive could claim to have tended it.

The fire in the stove ate the coal in a single silent breath.

It burned steadily — brighter than ever.

In the courtyard, the fire-seed tree shimmered in the new light.

And the morning began.
