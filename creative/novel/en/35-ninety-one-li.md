## Thirty-five: Ninety-one Li

From the Ember Post he walked the first ninety-one li the way his father's map had told him to — a straight blue-stone road through barley stubble and low ridges, the ink-shone box on his right shoulder, a canteen and half a dried loaf at his hip.

No one else was on the road. But every ninety-one li, he could see a broken pavilion standing alone.

The first pavilion — bare. Only a cold rice bowl and a scrap of paper under it, bearing one character: 火.

The second pavilion — a long bench, broken in the middle. On the table sat two apples, one gnawed, one whole. Plainly left behind for whoever came next.

The third pavilion — someone was there.

A woman not yet forty, at a pine table, grinding ink on a small slab. A single oil lamp burned between them. She did not turn when he came in. Only after he hung his kettle in front of the fire did she speak.

"So you're the Lin family's second son."

Lin Wen sat across from her. He did not correct her.

"I mean Lin Wen."

"Lin Wen — one who crosses," she said. "A name for someone who ferries others across." She finished grinding, picked up a brush, and wrote four characters on a slip of paper already waiting, and slid it across.

**Your father stayed here five nights.**

Lin Wen looked at her. He thought, suddenly, that his box had become lighter — the kind of heavier light you find when the thing you were meant to carry has been waiting for you all along.

"Auntie —" he began, and didn't know how to finish.

The woman inhaled softly. Her hair going grey at the temples; her skin burnished dark; her collar smudged with scorch-marks from old lamp-oil. She smiled.

"Call me Sister Gu," she said.

She gave him a bowl of hot broth and half a steamed bun. Lin Wen finally asked the question he had been carrying since the mountain:

"Sister Gu — this fire I haven't finished the reading of — what is my father owed?"

Sister Gu did not answer directly. She rose, trimmed the wick of the oil lamp, and lit a second flame.

"Look at the color," she said.

Lin Wen watched the flame. It burned blue at the rim, deep gold in the core — ordinary lamp-oil flame. Ordinary and extraordinary at once.

"The color of fire doesn't change," she said. "It hasn't changed in a hundred years either. Fire is easy to recognize — it never recognizes anyone."

She slid a black sheet across the table. It was identical to the ones in the box. The address across its head, in a cautious horizontal hand:

**Lin Wen.**

He took the sheet into his hands.

"Your father asked me to write this the night before he turned back at the fourth pavilion," she said. "He walked on for a while after that. Someone turned off his lamp at the fourth station — or he did. I never interfered, only tidied. I kept the fire until today."

"Are you 墨鹮?" Lin Wen asked suddenly.

She did not say yes and did not say no. She only moved the flame closer and passed the black sheet to him.

"If you can read what follows in the dark, walk it as it says. If you can't, go back to the Ember Post and hold it. Holding is also a kind of fire."

Lin Wen unfolded the sheet.

The deeper the lines were laid, the clearer they became:

*This road asks for a lamp every ninety-one li. Whoever forgets to light it will find, ninety-one li beyond it, that the road home can no longer be seen.*

On the far side of the ink stood a wall, and next to it, a door. Above the door only two characters, written by a hand worn almost beyond reading:

**火之门**

The Fire Gate.

Beneath that, a single clear stroke of black ink:

*The generation that reaches the gate — that is the generation the gate opens for.*

Lin Wen clasped the page. The wind came through the gap in the broken eaves and bent the lamp sideways until its shadow rippled on the wall — like an embrace that had not yet closed.

Sister Gu said: "Will you finish the bun, or not?"

He shook his head.

He stood. He took down the box, shouldered it, slid the black sheet inside. He paused in front of the reflection bent in the lamp-light.

In the fire, a shadow of a young man stood straight as a marker.

He did not look back. Instead, from the stove, Sister Gu drew one thing and pressed it into his palm — a small iron token about the size of a thumbnail, its rim rubbed perfectly smooth, as though it had been carried by more generations than these roads could count.

"When you reach the fourth pavilion, set the token down there," she said. "It will light itself."

Lin Wen closed his fingers around the token. He stepped out of the pavilion.

The north was a great wind and no moon. The road was the road it had always been. Each step he made, the token in his palm grew warmer.
