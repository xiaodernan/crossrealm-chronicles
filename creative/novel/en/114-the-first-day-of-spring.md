## One Hundred and Fourteen: The First Day of Spring

On the night before the first day of spring, a light snow fell on the courtyard.

It was not heavy — a few strong breaths of wind and it was nearly gone. The strip of frozen mud along the far edge of the yard was already turning dark.

At three in the morning, the courtyard gate rattled once.

Not the wind. Not the snow falling. A different sound.

A-Shi's eyes opened.

He sat up. He did not wake his mother. He went out to the door alone.

The door opened a crack. No snow came in — a figure stood in the doorway.

Not the old man from the mountain. Not the cooper. Not the letter-bearer.

A person whose face was hidden by a white cloth, fragrant with something he could not name.

The stranger drew from within his robe a small oil-paper parcel and handed it into the room. He said nothing. He turned and was gone.

A-Shi picked up the parcel.

Inside was — a single small piece of white birch. A fresh-looking one, still rimmed with frost, with a little snow clinging to the white.

His mother came to the doorway. One look was enough.

On the oil-paper was written four characters only:

"灶火春安。May the stove fire be at peace in spring."

She set the piece of white birch into the fire.

The flames shook once.

A-Shi placed his hand near the fire's edge. The warmth was intense — a spring kind of warmth.

"Stove," Xiao Huo said, rubbing his eyes.

"Stove," A-Shi confirmed.

The room went quiet.

"The stove ate a piece of white birch last night," his mother said.

Everyone in the house knew about the log of white birch Bai Yin had stored away six hundred years ago. It had been split into three pieces — the smallest had already gone into the stove in midwinter; the two larger pieces still sat in the yard.

Someone — someone no one had seen — had left this one on the doorstep.

The ones who came to leave white birch — they were folded into the stove's own kin. The fire knew all their names.

The morning of the first day of spring came up white and clean.

On the fire-seed tree, one tiny leaf pushed out — just one — and the stove lit it up like gold under the rising sun.

This house. Seven hundred years.

The first day of spring.

The stove is still burning.
