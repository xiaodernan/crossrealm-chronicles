# The Unchanted Chronicle of Crossrealm: The Keeper of the Anchor Fire

## Seventy-four: The Seed

The pine needles lay quiet in the wooden box for a full week.

On the seventh night, A-Shi could not hold back any longer. He waited until his mother was asleep, took out one of the needles, and placed it in the bare dirt beside the stove, watering it with a single spoonful of stove-ash slurry.

He told no one.

On the sixth morning, he woke early to feed the stove — and saw a tiny shoot pushing up through the soil.

Each sliver of green carried a faint orange rim, like a flame just coming awake. A breeze passed; the shoots shivered as if breathing.

A-Shi crouched before the seedling for a long while.

His mother came out to hang the washing. She saw the shoot, stopped still in the wind, and slowly lowered herself to one knee. She touched a single needle-tip and held it to her nose. She was silent for a long time before she finally said:

"It is a fire-seed tree."

"A fire-seed tree?"

She nodded.

"My mother took me up the mountain one summer, when I was a girl. She showed me one just like this. The needles were orange-red. She told me then it was the tree of the fire-keepers — that every keeper's house grows one, year after year."

She paused, then added:

"Six hundred years ago, it must have grown right here — at this very stove."

A-Shi looked at that tiny seedling, its orange rim trembling at the very edge of dawn.

That orange — he knew it. It was the colour at the final moment before a coal goes dark. The colour that leapt up only when an ember sparked. The colour that crested at the very tip of a flame as it licked the air above the stove.

"This seedling..." A-Shi said, "has not gone grey."

His mother laughed softly.

"Six hundred years," she said. "A seed, once planted, is never truly dead."

The seedling grew steadily — a few inches in its first month. Every morning A-Shi poured one spoonful of stove-ash water on the roots, never more, never less. He could not explain it — but he felt certain the seedling would not need his care for long. It would grow on its own.

Before the first early summer came around, a single cone appeared at the top — very small, no bigger than his thumb's nail, and all dark red. A-Shi looked at it for a long time, knowing one day this cone would fall, open, and send down six hundred more years of fire-trees through this very family's yard.

One night Baiyin came down from the ridge, stopped at the door, and asked to look at the stove. She saw the seedling and slowly lowered herself to the ground beside it.

She rubbed one needle-tip between her fingers and said:

"Fire-seeds."

She paused, and continued:

"Six hundred years ago, when he left the house, he carried three pine needles with him. Three needles, three trees. The other two are somewhere in the world — he carried them through every place he went. He left them where they were needed most."

"And the last one —"

Baiyin looked over at A-Shi.

"The last one — he left for you."

The stove-fire burned steadily.

Baiyin drew a pinch of ash from her sleeve and scattered it around the seedling's base.

"For you," she said. "Everything that came before you — every one of the six hundred years — belongs to this house now."

A-Shi took the ash carefully.

It was light, warm, and made no sound at all as it fell.

"Whenever your stove needs new firewood," Baiyin said, her voice calm as still water, "take a branch from this tree. Pine from the fire-seed tree burns longer and deeper than any wood. Six hundred years of the world, come to rest — it would be good for the stove."

She rose and walked out into the night without another word.

Summer came slowly. A-Shi returned the ash to the stove and attended to his daily chores. The next morning the fire lit more fiercely than it ever had before — and A-Shi was not certain whether it was his imagination, but the stove seemed quieter than it had in all the time since he was born.

Out beyond the gate, something small and enduring was growing quietly — as it had done for six hundred years.
