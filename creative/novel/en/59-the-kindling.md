## Fifty-nine: The Kindling

The approach of winter was in the air.

His mother built the stove first. She lifted a small piece of volcanic glass from her cupboard — a small rock, dark orange, cold to her fingertips.

She pressed it into A-Shi's palm.

"This," she said, "is the kindling. The whole hearth depends on this seed. Every time we raise a new flame, we touch it to this same stone. This is older than our family."

A-Shi held the stone carefully.

"How old?" he asked.

"Six hundred years," she said.

He did not understand.

"Six hundred years," she said again, "passed from one hand to the next, without ever breaking."

She replaced the stone in the wooden box and set the box back in the cupboard.

"It is why no one in our house has ever let the furnace grow cold," she said.

A-Shi stood watching her.

He stayed inside listening to the stove for a long time.

"Grandfather is in there, isn't he?" he asked finally. "In the fire?"

His mother did not answer.

She pointed to the stove.

The stove roared inward, deeper and deeper, as if answering something.

"He is not alone," she said. "No one ever was."

The fire crackled. Endless threads of heat curled upward.

A-Shi put his palm near the stove door.

Warm air pressed back against his hand.

His mother looked at what he was doing. She smiled.

"You already know," she said. "You always knew."

The room warmed around them both.

A-Shi nodded. He finally understood that his whole life had been inside this fire — every prayer he had ever not known he was making, every night he had sat by this hearth, every page of every book he had never read.

The fire was older than everyone.

The fire had never not known him.

A-Shi felt something turn inside him — not a feeling exactly — a direction.

He understood what it meant to be the next one to hold this.

"Then we will hold it together," he said.

His mother looked at him.

She nodded.

The stove kept burning.

No one in the house ever spoke of it after that.

They did not have to.

The fire spoke for everyone.
