## One Hundred and Six: The Stove Stories

Nine days after the old man had left, a cooper came to the house.

He was a silent man. He said he had come with an iron-banded bucket on his shoulder to fix the family's water cistern — in winter the cistern fed itself from the well, and by spring the rice pot and the stove pot were always one bowl short of water from each other.

When he had finished fixing the cistern he lingered in the courtyard a long time.

He noticed Xiao Huo cross the yard.

Xiao Huo was holding a piece of coal — the same coal A-Shi had brought down from the loft — turning it again and again in his two small hands. He ran from the woodpile to the stove, back to the woodpile, back to the stove. He said nothing. He just kept turning the coal over and over.

The cooper asked A-Shi: "Has this child been playing with a coal?"

A-Shi nodded.

"Why doesn't he speak?"

"He is thinking," A-Shi said.

The cooper nodded slowly.

"Mother," he called toward the kitchen, "may I hear the story of that night?"

A-Shi's mother stepped out. "What would you like to hear?"

"That night," the cooper said. "The story of the night all of you saw the fire."

Everyone sat down.

The stove burned quietly.

His mother asked A-Shi to tell it. He shook his head. Xiao Huo peeked in from the kitchen, darted his head back out again, peeked once more.

"Would you like to tell it?" his mother asked him.

Xiao Huo nodded. He climbed onto the little stool beside the stove and began.

"Three days before the midwinter," he said, "a thin snow fell in the courtyard."

"And then?" his mother asked gently.

"Then Mother spent three days oiling a paper umbrella — and it did not rain," Xiao Huo said.

"What rain?"

"Not the rain," he said. "Fire. The ancestor-fire came down the mountain."

The cooper felt a sudden warmth in his chest.

"Then the old man came," Xiao Huo went on. "He had a basket on his back. Inside the basket was a porcelain jar — a jar of ancestor-ash. And there was also a small ember, wrapped in white silk, held by a piece of iron wire."

"And then?" the cooper asked.

Xiao Huo thought hard for a moment.

"Then the old man put the ember into our stove," Xiao Huo said. "The stove leapt once. And it did not go out. In fact —" he paused — "it burned brighter than it had ever burned before."

No one in the room made a sound.

"Then the old man left," Xiao Huo continued. "And before he left he said one thing: 'The ancestor-fire has seen the stove. The stove has seen the ancestor-fire.'"

Xiao Huo was quiet again for a while. Then he added:

"So our fire is not ours. It is everyone's. It does not go out — not because one person keeps feeding it, but because so many people out there are waiting for it."

No one spoke.

The stove burned quietly.

The cooper stood up last of all.

"I would like to build a little stone marker at your gate," he said.

His mother said: "Do not put any word on it."

"Not even one word?" the cooper asked.

"Not one word," she said. "A written word becomes an altar. Our fire is not an altar. It belongs to the fire itself. No words."

The cooper was quiet for a long time.

Then he said: "All right. I will not carve one word. I will carve the shape of a single white birch branch."

His mother nodded.

"That is acceptable."

The stone marker went up quickly, on the south side of the courtyard, facing the road.

There were no words on it.

Only the carved outline of a single white birch branch.

When the rains soak the branch into the stone, moonlight catches the grain just right — and for a moment the branch seems to glow from inside the rock, like a low light coming up out of the earth.

After the cooper was gone, A-Shi stood with Xiao Huo in front of the new stone.

"What is this?" Xiao Huo asked.

"It is a kind of road," A-Shi said. "Anyone who walks into this courtyard and sees this white birch branch knows that somewhere here there is a fire — and that it has never once gone out in seven hundred years."

Xiao Huo reached out and laid his small hand on the cold stone.

In the kitchen, the stove burned steadily.

The stove.

Seven hundred years.

It has never once gone out.
