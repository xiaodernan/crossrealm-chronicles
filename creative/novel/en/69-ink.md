# The Unchanted Chronicle of Crossrealm: The Keeper of the Anchor Fire

**Adapted from**: Crossrealm Chronicles
**Volume XI**: Ink Fire — the world six hundred years on

## Sixty-nine: Ink

On his first watch before the hearth, after the gathering, the wind changed.

It came in from beyond the ridge, carrying a very faint smell — like fresh rain, or like cold ash. He thought he was dreaming, but at first light the flame of the hearth had shrunk by an inch, as if something had pressed it gently down.

He assumed the wood was damp and fetched a dry log. The fire still lay flat.

"So you saw it," came a voice from behind the stove.

A-Shi started. Baiyin had been there the whole time, sitting in the dark — moon-white robe, half her face lit by the dying ember.

"Saw what?"

"Ink."

He did not understand. He added another dry log. This time the flame lifted a little, and sank again slowly.

Baiyin leaned toward the stove, her face tipped into the firelight.

"Six hundred years ago, when the first flame was kindled, this world had colour. Red was red. Azure was azure. Sunsets were actually burning. Later —" she paused, "— later, a character fell."

"Which character?"

Baiyin did not answer at once. She lowered herself to the hearthside and held out her palm over the fire. The light stained the back of her hand with a dark orange pattern, like rice paper scorched by a candle.

"Ink," she said. "Not a stain — a word. This world is pinned under a single character — 墨. Ink is not dirt. Ink is what remains when everything burnt — wood becomes ink, bones become ink, cruel words become ink. And this flame," she pointed at the stove, "is one of the very few lights ink cannot swallow."

A-Shi stared into the hearth. The fire burned on, apparently unchanged, yet somehow brighter than it had been all night — a faint light that had been hiding six hundred years in plain sight.

"Then why do we guard it?" he asked.

Baiyin looked at him.

"To remember."

"To remember what?"

"To remember us — to remember what fire looked like. When this world is finally pinned under ink and all its colours are drawn back in, there will need to be someone who remembers what fire was."

A-Shi was silent for a long time.

"Was that what the six hundred years of keepers were for?"

Baiyin nodded.

"They didn't keep fire for the fire's sake," she said. "They kept it because the Chinese character for warmth — 暖 — has a fire inside it. Without the fire, there is no warmth. Without warmth there are no people."

Then she drew from her sleeve a slim booklet so thin it was nearly invisible — its corners charred black. She opened it on the low table beside the stove.

Every page was covered in handwriting. Line upon line, stroke after stroke — all of them the same character: 记 — to remember. Some strokes slanted, some were carved with fingernails, some were pressed so hard the paper had given way beneath them.

"Six hundred years," Baiyin said, "every keeper, on their deathbed, writes one character in here. However long they kept the fire, it gets remembered. You'll do the same, one day."

A-Shi reached out and touched the first stroke. The ink was deep, impossibly deep. He could not see the hand that had written it — yet under his fingertips, somehow, he felt warmth. Somebody — a keeper from six hundred years ago — had left that warmth pressed into the page.

"It's all fire," A-Shi whispered.

Baiyin smiled.

"Yes," she said. "It's all the same character, the same flame, the same warmth. Six hundred years kept, and six hundred more still to keep."

The log in the hearth cracked. A single spark leapt up and landed beside the open page. It did not burn the paper. It left only a tiny glowing circle.

Baiyin closed the booklet and pressed it into A-Shi's hands.

"Take it. After six hundred years, the fire finally lands in your keeping."

She walked to the door. She did not turn back, but her last words reached him clearly across the room:

"Remember this — ink is the character the world will one day close around. Fire is the warmth the world will one day burn straight through. Your work is to make sure the two things remember each other."

A-Shi sat before the stove for what must have been hours.

Dawn came slowly. The hearth burned on, steady, like a letter sent from six hundred years ago — and on the envelope only two characters were written:

Keep it.
