## One Hundred and Twelve: The Night of the Vigil

On the day of the winter solstice, the courtyard had no daylight at all.

By nine in the morning it was light, but by three in the afternoon the sky was already pitch black. Every branch of the fire-seed tree was silhouette against frost, and the one warm thing in the yard was the tofu-cube glow of the stove at the center of the kitchen.

His mother made a small supper — nothing abundant — this house had learned to live on cabbage and tofu since autumn's first cool mornings.

But tonight was different.

Tonight the stove was not to go out.

His mother called Xiao Huo to the stove.

"For seven hundred years," she said, "whoever was in the family would take turns watching the fire. But tonight — no one takes turns."

"And A-Shi?"

"A-Shi is asleep," she said. "Tonight A-Shi lets you take the watch."

Xiao Huo sat at the stove. The fire leaned toward him, the way it always leaned toward warm hands.

He reached out to touch the three characters carved against the stove iron — 火 · 灶 · 守 — each stroke worn a little smoother by the heat of six and a half centuries.

The stove was steady.

"Bank it lightly," she said. "And keep the ash level."

He knelt on the hearth stones and took the long iron spoon in his small hand. He raked the coals until the white ash lay flat and even, and the buried embers glowed even at their edges.

All night, wind pressed against the door. Every two or three hours it rattled the latch — once, twice, once.

Xiao Huo watched the fire. He fed it one sliver of birch at a time — slow, sparing, steady. He never let it grow too high. He never let it fall too low.

He never fell asleep.

At three in the morning, the wind stopped. Every sound in the courtyard went silent.

At four, the horizon began to turn white.

At five, the branches of the fire-seed tree came back into relief.

At six, the gate rattled — the same rattle they had come to know. His mother, dressed in white, came out and stood in the courtyard.

"The stove?" she said.

"Still burning," Xiao Huo said.

She went inside. She looked at the warm hearth, at the level ash, at Xiao Huo standing beside the stove in his small night-robe.

She said nothing for a long time.

Then she bowed — to Xiao Huo.

Not the way elders bow to children — the way a keeper bows to a keeper.

In the hearth, one piece of birch he did not remember feeding sat glowing at the edge — the one A-Shi had tucked in before lying down at midnight and told nobody to tell him about.

That was the night.

Seven hundred years of keepers watching this fire — and no one had ever watched it alone. Tonight was the first time.

Tonight the fire had an entrusted watcher.

At dawn, she kissed the top of his head.

"Happy solstice," she said.

Xiao Huo did not answer. He was too tired, and too proud.

He banked the fire again, the way he had been taught.

The stove was very quiet.

Seven hundred years, every night someone had said "tomorrow" — until tonight.

Tonight he said "tomorrow" himself.

And meant it.
