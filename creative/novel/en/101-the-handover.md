## One Hundred and One: The Handover

On the fifth day of the seven-hundredth year, it rained.

Not a storm — a fine, powdery rain that fell from morning to dusk, beading on the needles of the fire-seed tree, on the blue tiles of the stove-house, on the stone steps, on every surface that six hundred years of stove-warmth had ever touched.

When the rain stopped, A-Shi's mother called them both to the stove.

She was holding a small iron box.

It was very old. The rust had grown a fine, dark patina over the iron, and inside the patina were six hundred years of smoke, six hundred years of ash, six hundred years of the warmth of hands. A-Shi could tell that the box was older than anything he had ever seen — older than the fire-seed tree in the courtyard, older than the great beam of the kitchen, older even than the stories his grandmother's grandmother had told.

"Seven hundred years," his mother said. "It is time to pass it on."

She did not open the box. She set it on the stove, where the fire could see it.

The fire lit the box. The box glowed a little.

Xiao Huo stared at it with wide eyes.

His mother said: "A-Shi. Tend the stove from now on."

A-Shi was the keeper of this generation — the six-hundred-and-second in seven hundred years. He had been the one to crouch at the stove, to feed it, to bank it at night and welcome it in the morning. He had taken the fire from his mother's hands at twenty-three. He was now forty-one. In all that time, only one sentence had ever been said to him: the fire does not go out; the people do not scatter.

He did not move.

His mother smiled — a long smile that carried seven hundred years of similar evenings, similar stillness, similar almost-imperceptible hesitation from other keepers before him.

"From today on," she said, "you teach him."

A-Shi lowered his head.

Xiao Huo was not yet six. He squatted in front of the stove, face tilted upward, watching his grandmother, his grandfather, the fire, the box. His fingers were stained with ink — yesterday he had taken A-Shi's brush and drawn a single small flame on the kitchen wall, perfectly round.

A-Shi looked at that round little flame and understood.

Handing on the fire was never about passing a flame from one hand to another.

It was about handing on a memory that would never go out: the seed of fire that had fallen from the white stone on the ridge onto the first stove, into the first pair of hands, into the first whispered promise — the fire does not go out; the people do not scatter. The fire remembered every keeper who had tended it. The stove had memorized every silence. What they were handing down was a single thread: one person, one generation, one courtyard, one tree, one ancestor of the fire on the ridge — all saying, together, to seven hundred years of time: we are still here.

"All right," A-Shi said.

His mother slid the iron box across the stove toward Xiao Huo.

"Open it," she said.

Xiao Huo stretched out two small hands. He pushed twice. The lid would not budge. He pushed harder. The box sat perfectly still.

His mother glanced at A-Shi.

A-Shi came over and knelt. He turned the box face-up and found a small hidden latch — fused with six hundred years of smoke until it opened only when pressed at exactly the right place.

He pressed.

The lid opened with a soft click.

Inside there was no gold, no jade, no celestial script. There was a stack of yellowed paper, and on top of it a single piece of black charcoal — the same kind A-Shi had found in the loft — but older, so old that it crumbled at a touch.

The words on the topmost page were written by the first keeper.

They were narrow, straight, and steady.

A-Shi read them aloud, one by one:

"Fire fell from the white stone. I caught it. I do not know where it came from, and I do not know where it is going. I know only one thing — it is afraid of the cold. If I do not feed it, it will go out. If it goes out, this courtyard will go dark, this pot will cool, and the porridge in this pot will lose its fragrance. So I feed it. Every day. Even when the wind comes. Even when the rain comes. Even when I am too sick to stand, I crawl out of bed and I feed it — because this is my duty. The fire owes me nothing. I owe the fire. It passes down through one generation after another, and I walk one step further each time it is handed over. Whoever is reading these words in the seven-hundredth year — the fire is still burning."

A-Shi stopped.

His mother did not speak. Xiao Huo did not speak. The fire inside the stove leapt gently once.

A-Shi read on:

"To whoever comes after me, if you have found this page — you are the one in the seven-hundredth year. The fire in the stove is still burning. The ancestor-flame on the white stone is still burning too. I do not know how long they will burn. I know only this — as long as someone still feeds the fire, it will never go out. When you feed the fire, feed one extra piece of wood for me. My name was —"

The words stopped here.

A star had burned a corner of the page, and the final character had been reduced to half a radical.

His mother saw the half-radical and her tears fell without warning.

"Bai," she said.

A-Shi looked up.

"The one who received the fire seven hundred years ago had the surname Bai," she said. "Her name was Bai Yin. Her seed fell in this stove. It fell in this fire-seed tree. It fell in this courtyard, and into your hands, and into today —"

She looked at Xiao Huo.

"— into his hands."

Xiao Huo looked down at his own small hands. They were still small. His palms carried soot, ink, and one short hair that had been singed by a spark.

That evening, A-Shi put the charcoal back in the box. He laid one more page on top of it — one he had written that very afternoon.

Xiao Huo, crouched beside the table, stole glances at the characters.

He could not make out most of it. He only understood two of the words: "continue writing."

The fire-seed tree in the courtyard swayed lightly in the night breeze, its needles still wet with rain. Far off on the ridge, the ancestor-flame burned low and steady. On the stove, the fire burned one inch higher.

The fifth day of the seven hundredth year.

The fire does not go out.

The people do not scatter.
