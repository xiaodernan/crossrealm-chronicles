## Twenty-nine: The First Cicada

The first cicada of July called on the day the seedling put out its sixth leaf.

Lin Wen was up on the roof that morning, re-setting loose tiles on the house's southwest corner ahead of the rainy season. Crouched on the ridge, he heard it come up from the slope — once, then again — a sound like a very small door being pushed open, then very gently pulled shut.

He climbed down and went to look at the seedling.

It was two feet tall now. The sixth leaf had just unfurled and was still curled at the edges, its pale color leaning toward pink. The bamboo sleeve held firm; the red cord on the stake had been burnished by the wind until it gleamed. Around the rim of the filled-in hole he counted six sets of footprints: his, his mother's, his sister's, his nephew's, the neighbor's, and one more from the morning his mother came down to check on it again.

His mother watered the seedling by her own rules: once every second day, at the root before the height of summer, a fine spray on the leaves only — never the soil — after the heat came. A persimmon must not be spoiled, she said. It was her mother's rule before her, and the one she used on the old tree by the gate.

August came in like an iron kiln. The leaves on the old tree curled at the edges, spotted here and there with scorch, as if a nail had pressed them into little pale coins. Every evening his mother hauled up a bucket and splashed cold water across the trunk — never the ground, only the tree.

"The tree isn't hot," she said. "The soil is hot. Remember that."

In the middle of August, a sudden storm broke over the slope. The rain bent the young seedling low, and Lin Wen raced up the hill to find the bamboo sleeve half washed away and the red cord grinding deep into the stake's split. He reset the stake that night and coaxed the curled sixth leaf open with a sliver of bamboo.

His mother watched him work from at the foot of the slope. When the sun came out, she went back to the house and returned with a length of old blue cloth with a tiny persimmon blossom embroidered at one corner. She tied it to the top of the frame.

"Sunshade," she said.

Lin Wen thought it was simply a scarf. She said it was not. It had belonged to her own mother — the grandmother Lin Wen had never seen in any photograph. She reached to steady the knot and then stopped herself.

"What the branch wants is light," she said. "What the child wants is light. But the child still needs someone to stand between it and the midday sun until it is strong enough to bear it."

The September wind dried gradually. The seedling stood a full three and a half feet high, leaf upon leaf drawn into a dense emerald crown — visibly smaller than the old tree, but unmistakably brighter. The old tree's leaves shifted to gold, and one or two at the very edge of its crown had already begun to let go.

Lin Wen wanted to feed the old tree. His mother refused.

"When we grow old," she said, "we don't eat what the little ones eat."

That evening he hauled two buckets of water up from the river to splash the old trunk. His sister called, and the child asked — she relayed the question through her mother — whether the new tree could bear fruit. Lin Wen looked at the gate tree — twenty-three persimmons this year, one fewer than the summer before. Then he looked at the streak of green on the slope, which had never borne anything in its life.

"Yes," he said into the phone. "When it grows up."

He hung up and looked toward the slope. The autumn wind rose, and the new tree's leaves made a long soft rush, like a bird not yet ready to fly, beating its wings against the quiet, waiting.

He counted forward in his head — starting today. One year, five years, six. One more, perhaps two. Then he would see the first fruit set on the new tree. And that fruit would fall, and his mother——

He stopped. He did not let himself finish the thought.

That evening the fire in the stove burned steadily. His mother lifted persimmons out of a pot where they had been simmering into a deep red paste. The most perfect one — the one that would hold its shape — she set on a fresh dish and placed it beside the old empty altar bowl.

This one was new.
