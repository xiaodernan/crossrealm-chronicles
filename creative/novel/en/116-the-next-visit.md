## One Hundred and Sixteen: The Next Visit

On the twelfth day after the spring equinox, the first guest of the season came to the house.

Not the old man from the mountain.

It was the neighbor, old Li.

There were only three households next to them. Old Li's family kept six mouths to feed. He stood at the door with a slightly embarrassed look.

"The fire," he said, "the fire — would it come to our house tomorrow?"

A-Shi stared at him for a long moment.

His mother came out of the kitchen.

Old Li wrung his hands. "Our stove at home — it doesn't work as well as yours. I heard you say it before — your fire, it's alive. Our fire, it's not alive."

He said "alive" with a lot of confidence. A-Shi did not contradict him.

"If it's all right with you," A-Shi said, "you can come see it tomorrow."

"See it?" Old Li took one step sideways.

"The stove," A-Shi said, "doesn't really leave. But you can come over and eat here if you want."

Old Li shifted his stance again. "I'll come," he said finally.

The next day, the weather turned. A real rain — thunder rolling from far off, the street below the house streaming red-brown with water.

At breakfast time, the gate rattled once.

Old Li stood under the eave, one sleeve already wet.

The whole family stood.

His mother ushered him inside.

The stove was burning steadily.

Old Li stood before the fire. He stood very properly, the way someone behaves when he's worried he'll touch something fragile.

His mother asked A-Shi to serve the rice.

Old Li could not sit down. He kept looking at the fire, then down at his own feet.

"I can't do this," he said finally — very soft, almost to himself.

No one laughed.

A-Shi brought two bowls of noodles and set them on the low table.

"Stove," Old Li said, barely audible.

"Mm," A-Shi said.

"I'll come back," Old Li said. "I'll come back tomorrow."

He said nothing more. He left.

The gate swung shut behind him.

The fire-seed tree shook.

"He was scared," Xiao Huo said after the gate closed.

"He wasn't," his mother said.

"He was really scared."

His mother did not reply.

She washed Old Li's bowl and turned it over on the shelf.

The stove burned steady as ever.

"Next time," A-Shi's mother said, "let him come."

The room was quiet.

Outside the gate, the fire-seed tree stirred.

Seven hundred years, and for the first time the neighbor's hearth and this hearth had eaten at the same table.

The fire had never seen this many people sit with it at once.

It had not gone out.

It had not gone out.

Spring had come for keeps.
