## One Hundred and Five: The Midwinter Visit

Three days before the midwinter, a light snow fell on the courtyard.

It was not heavy — a thin white dusting on the needles of the fire-seed tree, bending the branches once, twice, before they sprang back. The stone steps vanished under the snow; A-Shi swept them clean three times a day.

His mother spent three days oiling the paper umbrella — she intended, she did not say, for Xiao Huo.

Xiao Huo did not understand. "Do we still need an umbrella in the snow?"

His mother smiled. "Not for this snow. For something coming soon."

The house grew quieter than it ever had. Even the stove seemed to settle into a patient silence — every piece of white birch in it burned to completion with a single sharp pop at the end, as if confirming an answer. No stray flames leapt up without cause. The fire was waiting.

On the fourth morning, as the sky turned pearl, a figure appeared at the bend of the path.

He had no horse, no cart — only a single basket slung across his back. It was slung very high, nearly to the top of his head. He walked one step, then stopped, then walked one more, then stopped again — the load was heavy.

Xiao Huo saw him first. He stood up, ran two steps toward the gate — stopped, ran back — stopped again.

A-Shi was already standing outside.

It was the same old man from the mountain.

He stepped into the courtyard, and knelt on one knee.

So did A-Shi's mother.

This time there was no letter. This time there was the basket.

Xiao Huo squatted beside the stove and stared at the basket.

It was woven from bamboo, the base lined with thin iron plate. Inside it sat a small porcelain jar — Xiao Huo could not yet name it, but A-Shi knew it: a jar of ancestor-ash. Yet something else hung above it — a fine iron wire, and at its tip, a tiny ball of living charcoal.

The old man glanced at A-Shi.

A-Shi nodded.

The old man set the porcelain jar on the stove. The stove flame leapt — one inch higher than it had ever burned.

Xiao Huo held his breath.

His mother rose and offered her chair. The old man would not sit. He stood. And he did not look at the stove — he looked at Xiao Huo.

Xiao Huo asked: "How long will you stay?"

"One night," the old man said.

"One night?"

"One night," he said. "The ancestor-fire wants to see the stove. Six hundred years, it has never seen the stove. It wants to look."

He reached into his robe and drew out something wrapped in cloth. He opened it carefully — inside it, a tiny square of white silk, and on the silk, a single living ember, no bigger than a chestnut, glowing soft and red.

The old man held the ember in both hands.

A-Shi's mother held out an iron basin.

A-Shi parted the stove's coals until only the white-hot, clean bottom lay exposed.

The old man bent and laid the ember inside.

The stove leapt once — and did not diminish. It blazed brighter than it ever had in seven hundred years.

Both keepers wept, without a sound.

Xiao Huo stared at the two fires burning side by side and whispered something no one could make out. He was very quiet afterward, and at last fell asleep where he sat.

The fire lit his face. The snow had stopped. The fire-seed tree stirred gently in the cold wind.

The old man left at dawn. As he turned at the bend of the path, he called back, only once:

"The ancestor-fire has seen the stove. The stove has seen the ancestor-fire."

That night, A-Shi's mother brought out the two remaining pieces of Bai Yin's white birch and stood them on either side of the stove — not burned, not separated, just set up as witnesses.

The stove burned for one full night.

No piece of white birch leapt in by itself.

The stove knew. It did not need to leap in new offerings anymore — tonight, it was two fires living in one hearth.
