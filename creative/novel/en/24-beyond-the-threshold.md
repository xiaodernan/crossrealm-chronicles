## Twenty-four: Beyond the Threshold

When Lin Wen stood at the door, he already knew that what lay beyond was not his own village — it could not be.

The door did not open for him. The door was open already.

Beyond the door was a cobbled road. He stood for a long time looking down its length: the road did not know where the maple forest at the far end opened onto, and the forest did not know what map it would eventually become.

He walked a few uncertain steps before realising that the persimmon tree at the village mouth behind him had grown taller than he remembered.

He lifted his head and looked outward — a vast moon had risen over the ridge, washing the whole valley in silver. It was the same moon that kept returning, one way or another, in pages behind this one: every page had mentioned it, but no one had ever suspected why it always came at the right time.

He counted the silver light-spots on the road. When he reached the seventh one, he stopped. Not because he lost count — the spots had arranged themselves on the ground into a single Chinese character: 看。

*Look.* The moon had not spoken; it only illuminated the shape so clearly that Lin Wen could not pretend not to see it.

Lin Wen slipped the book back into his coat and knelt at the seventh stain of silver light. In the center of it lay a small persimmon — the exact one his mother used to pick, and the very one the tree had dropped for the last time.

He ate it. It was sweet, but not as sweet as he remembered, because this time he was the one doing the remembering, not the sweetness itself.

Lin Wen continued down the cobbled road. Another silver spatter appeared on his sleeve, spelling a second character: 回。

*Return.* Lin Wen understood at last why the door had never been nailed nor truly opened.

It was not because the door wanted to remain shut. The door had only ever been waiting for one who could truly *return*. This was not a person who had wandered from elsewhere — it was someone who had been there all along.

Lin Wen closed the book. The wind pressed gently through the distant persimmon. He lifted the book and held it out — to the reader on the other side of this page.

He looked for a long moment, then said simply:

"You may keep this book. Do not lock it in a drawer. Every time you open it, remember it is the fire that keeps the pages warm."
