## Seventy: The Six Hundred and First

On the morning Baiyin left, A-Shi found the stove-fire dimmed to a single thread.

He thought it was a matter of wood. He fed it three dry logs, but the flame stayed weak. The red coals in the ash lay curled like a tiny, drowsy orange.

He climbed the ridge at a run. The night-lamp on the white stone had gone out.

New snow lay over the stone, bright as a mirror. The glass shell was filmed with frost. The wick showed its last burn — black, curled into a tiny hook, like a copper wire bent shut. Six hundred years, and this lamp had never gone out. He had lit it himself on that autumn night, and Baiyin had sat beside it all night, waiting for it to brighten again.

"It's out," he whispered.

From his belt he untied the kindle-jar — the one they used every year for the kindle rite — and squatted beside the stone for a long while, searching for something he could not name.

A step behind him. Baiyin stood there, her white robe whipping in the winter wind, her face the calmest it had ever been.

"It's supposed to be out," she said.

"Why?"

Baiyin beckoned him closer.

"Come."

A-Shi stopped three steps away from her, the old habit of caution still in his bones. She did not ask him to step any nearer. She only said:

"For six hundred years, that lamp did not fail. It wasn't the fire's doing — it was the people's. Your grandfather, your great-grandfather, your great-great-grandfather — they all kept watch on this lamp through the night. They knew the fire would not go out. They only could not bear not to look."

She stared at the lamp-stand, and her voice lowered to almost nothing:

"But your stove is different. Your stove is the thing that does not go out. It hastaken in all the six hundred years of warmth. What burns in that stove is the thing that actually survived."

"Then the lamp...?"

"Was a beacon — to show the valley that someone up here was watching. Six hundred years, the whole village knew: the peak is still lit, so nobody needs to be afraid. It does not need to burn anymore, because —"

She looked down the ridge, toward the village.

"Because your stove stands now, A-Shi. For the first time in six hundred years, the valley does not need the lamp on the peak to feel safe."

A-Shi stared at the dead wick.

Baiyin continued:

"The night-lamp is the last of its kind. It is handed down to your stove now. Anyone in the valley who looks up to the ridge will no longer see a light — but they will know the chimney from that house in the east never stops. That is enough."

"And it — ?" A-Shi gestured at the lamp.

Baiyin held out her palm. On it sat one last pinch of black ash — warm, somehow, glowing with six hundred years' final breath.

"Take it to your stove. Let it join the fire there."

A-Shi received the ash in both hands, as carefully as he had received the ember from Aunty Li's incense pot. When he was six, he had held Grandfather's charcoal brazier in just this way, and it had only warmed him for a single winter. This time —

This time he was holding the close of six hundred years.

He walked down the ridge, unstoppered the kindle-jar above the stove, and poured the dust slowly into the hearth. The fire did not leap. It only paused for half a heartbeat, then grew a little brighter — as if the six hundred years had answered.

His mother opened her eyes. Seeing the stove burn like this, she smiled.

Baiyin sat down in the doorway.

"The night-lamp is out?" she asked.

A-Shi nodded.

His mother laughed softly. "The seven-hundredth year, then."

Baiyin shook her head.

"The six hundred and first," she said. "These six hundred years, every keeper passed his fire down to the next. Tonight, it comes all to A-Shi. From today onward, the stove is a light the whole world can see."

She turned toward A-Shi.

"Do you know what feast today is?"

He did not.

Baiyin said:

"The ascension of the six hundred and first keeper."

She said it gravely, with none of her usual lightness, as though the first six hundred years were standing behind her.

A-Shi lowered his head. He did not know what to say. He could see the stove-fire shining in everyone who had ever stood watch over this hearth — Grandfather's profile, Father's open hand, Baiyin's eyes, Uncle Ye's pipe smoke, the old Daoist's sandals on the threshold, his mother's young face.

"From tomorrow," Baiyin said, "the beacon is no longer the lamp. You are."

Then she stood and walked to the doorway.

Before she stepped through, she turned and said one sentence — and for all his life afterward A-Shi could never recall the whole of it, only the last half:

"— Fire is not kept for people to see. Fire is kept so that people can remember. Remember it, and it will not go out."

She left. She did not look back, and she did not say goodbye — the way every keeper before him had come into his life, or been from it.

A-Shi reached for another stick and set it inside the stove. The sparks leapt high and filled the hearth.

"I will remember," he said.

The fire answered.
