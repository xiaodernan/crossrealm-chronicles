## Forty-nine: Five Years From Now

After 墨曦 left, the quiet returned to the slope, just as it had arrived.

Lin Wen did not mention her; A-Shi did not ask about her.

The summer rolled away. One afternoon a courier rode in on a scrawny mare and stopped at the gate, carrying only a small parcel. What weighed in the pouch was lighter than a kite.

Lin Wen opened it in front of the stove. Inside was a paper-wrapped box of fresh-made matches — red-ended, well dipped — and a note with only one line:

**阿石。五年后用。**

No signature, no date, no postage mark. Just the matches.

His mother took one look at the box and was still for a long time.

"These are not ordinary matches," she said.

A-Shi knelt beside her with his head between his shoulders.

"They belong to the patriarch," his mother said. "Or they belong to the one Kok starts with."

A-Shi put the matchbox in his pocket. He walked to the threshold and looked out toward the distant ridge. He thought he saw the line on the horizon where Du's old courtyard would be.

Something stirred.

"These matches came from 墨鹮," Lin Wen said quietly, standing behind. "From your grandfather's time."

A-Shi did not answer. He put his hand into his pocket and closed his fingers around the matchbox.

"Five years is a long time," Lin Wen said.

A-Shi said nothing.

But his mother could see it — that steady light in his eye had never gone out. He had simply changed its color.

"Then five years we hold them," his mother said. "But five years cannot end without the first match."

The wind turned. The eastern slope exhaled.

Springs passed. A-Shi went to school, came home, helped carry water, held the broom.

Every year some new persimmon leaves arrived on the outside porch rail.

Every year one old leaf fell off.

No one asked him what he kept inside that matchbox.

In the fifth year, on the night of the spring equinox — the longest day — A-Shi walked out into the courtyard with the matchbox in his pocket. The stove was cold. The persimmon tree was bare.

He pulled the box from his pocket.

He took one match by its head.

He struck it.

The flame leapt up.

The whole courtyard lit up — the old stones, the persimmon branches, the withered sapling frame, the bowl of leaves, the empty bowl from his mother's table.

He heard his mother's voice from the doorway.

Not shouting — a low, low breath of words only:

"A-Shi. The fire is lit. Even after five years it is the same one his father left you."

A-Shi straightened his back.

He carried the match into the stove and let the flame spread across the charcoal.

He could feel it — the light in the room growing brighter, smaller, sharper — until it seemed to find him from old walls.

Far down-mount the eastern ridge — thousands of miles away — one thin column of smoke rose, perpendicular to the horizon. Someone, somewhere, was very far away, very old, and probably alive.

Maybe not anyone — maybe only one young man, walking south with a smoking stick on his shoulder.

Maybe no one at all.

But the fire in A-Shi's stove never flickered again.
