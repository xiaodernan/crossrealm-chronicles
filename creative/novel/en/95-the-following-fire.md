## Ninety-five: The Following Fire

Three months after Baiyin's last visit, a letter came.

The letter was almost empty — nothing but a single line of text across the middle.

A-Shi held the paper to the stove to read it in the orange light — and discovered, as he tilted the page, that there was no ink on it at all.

But the paper was warm.

Not warm from his hands or from the kitchen stove. It was warm because it had been carrying something inside it — something that had not yet become anything a reader could recognise as a letter.

The fire had written it.

A whole six hundred years of keepers had signed their names — had written wordlessly — and the ink had never left the paper.

Mother walked in, soapsuds on her hands, and saw him standing with the letter open.

She noticed immediately.

"That paper," she said. "It was never written on."

He nodded.

"The fire wrote it without writing."

She dried her hands with a kitchen cloth. She set it down.

"Everyone who ever tended this stove," she said, "told it their names. The stove carried them. The stove carried you. The stove is carrying you."

She reached into the fireplace — not into the fire, but beside it, in the folded wool on the shelf — and drew out something wrapped in three layers of cloth.

She unwrapped it.

Inside lay a small rough-hewn skillet — the same shape as the one resting inside the hearth, the same kind of iron as the iron of the stove itself. The iron was still warm.

She placed it in his hands.

"Six hundred years of fire," she said gently.

He could not speak.

She looked at him steadily.

"Seven hundred years," she said.

Xiao Huo, who had been listening from the doorway, toddled forward and nestled the skillet beside him against A-Shi's chest.

The stove burned quietly in the background.

The eastern beacon burned across the ridge.

Grandmother was not absent.

She had been here all along.
