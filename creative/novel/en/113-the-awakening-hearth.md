## One Hundred and Thirteen: The Awakening Hearth

The next morning, fog rolled in from outside the courtyard.

It was thick the way porridge is thick — the doorway vanished into it. Every needle on the fire-seed tree was furred with frost, and the frost gleamed where the fog pressed against it, like an extra set of white candles.

His mother was already cooking — cabbage, tofu, the last of the rice.

The stove was burning steadily, the way it had burned all night.

A-Shi woke. He had not slept at all the night before.

Xiao Huo woke.

He sat up slowly. He looked at the stove. It was still burning — same level, same calm, same golden yellow it had been at three in the morning when the wind had stopped for an instant.

He said nothing.

A-Shi stood in the doorway with his back to the room. He said nothing.

The stove crackled twice — one soft pop, one lower roll deeper down in the coal bed.

"It knows me now," A-Shi said finally. His voice was lower than ordinary.

His mother did not turn around. She only nodded.

The fog kept coming.

The fire-seed tree swayed somewhere in it, grey needles whispering against grey air. Spring was still a fortnight away, but the wind already carried one extra degree of warmth.

Xiao Huo knelt at the stove. He looked at the fire a long time.

He reached out and placed one small hand flat against the iron edge. The iron was warm — not hot, exactly, but warm in the way iron is warm when the fire beneath it has burned steady all night.

"Last night was me," he said.

No one answered.

Out in the fog, a crow called once.

A-Shi knelt slowly beside Xiao Huo at the stove. He did not look at his brother, nor at his mother. He looked at the fire — the way a person looks at a road home.

Then he reached out both hands and rested them on the stove's edge.

"Seven hundred years," he said.

His hands were lit red-gold by the fire.

His mother came to stand behind them both. She reached out and folded a corner of her apron over the stove's edge — not to touch it, just to be close to it.

"From now on," she said, "the two of you will keep the fire. In turns."

She paused.

"Whoever keeps it knows."

The kitchen was silent for a long time.

The stove crackled once more — a single, deliberate pop, the way a stove answers when it is ready.

From that day forward, nobody in the house used the words "keeping the fire."

The stove had become the third member of the family.

Out in the yard, the reeds had browned.

Spring was still a fortnight away.

But already here, already close, already in every warm square of light this stove poured into the kitchen.

Spring was coming.

The stove was ready for it.
