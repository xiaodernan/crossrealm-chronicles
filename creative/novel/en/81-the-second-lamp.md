# The Unchanted Chronicle of Crossrealm: The Keeper of the Anchor Fire

**Genre**: Planar fantasy / dark epic / metafiction
**Adapted from**: Crossrealm Chronicles
**Volume XII**: The Long Journey — Fire and Ink

## Eighty-one: The Second Lamp

A-Shi walked on north.

All day he crossed a vast ash-flat — no trees, no birds — only a straight raised road running toward the horizon. Gray stone markers stood at intervals along the verge, each engraved with two faint characters: 守火.

He read one, and walked on.

Toward sunrise on the second morning, a lamp appeared in the distance.

It burned small and steady, the same amber flame he knew from the ever-burning lamp at the crossroads and the one at his own stove-side. As he drew closer, he saw a person sitting beside it.

A young woman — about his age — in white robes with quiet, level brows, straining a basket of dried rice straw before the lamp's warmth.

She looked up without haste.

"Are you from the south?" she asked.

A-Shi nodded.

She smiled, then reached into her sleeve and drew out a small wooden plaque, identical to the one she was holding herself — engraved in tiny characters:

守火 — 守到全案。

Tend the fire — until it has reached all.

"Are you the fire of the old grandmother?" she asked.

A-Shi stared.

"Do you know her?"

The woman nodded.

"I am her younger sister," she said. "This family has kept three lamps — your one in the south, this one in the east, and one more that has been dark six hundred years to the far north. We all three grew up tending the same borrowed fire."

Her words fell into the wind and stayed there.

A-Shi said nothing for a long time.

The wind tugged gently at the second lamp, carrying the smell of wheat husk and ash.

"You have come just in time," she said.

She drew out a small black stone — exactly like the coal A-Shi had taken from the broken white stone, the same black kindling-stock.

"This lamp has not been lit for six hundred years," she said. "Your stove is the one that has never gone out. The flame you carry is the only blood all of them share."

He opened his palm to look at the stone.

She laid it in his hand.

"Six hundred years ago, your great-great-grandfather gave this fire to someone going the other way. That person passed it on to his own family — and they to theirs — until the wind reached us."

A-Shi held the stone loosely.

The woman added softly:

"Take it with you. Six hundred years is too long to wait for someone to come home."

A-Shi shook his head — slowly, firmly.

"I am A-Shi," he said.

She smiled.

"A-Shi needs no introduction. I have smelled your fire for six hundred years."

He smiled back for the first time on the road.

She gathered up her straw and motioned toward the north.

"When the sun touches the ridge, the third lamp will show faint light. It has been dark for six hundred years — no one has tried to light it since."

She looked at him.

"Everything you have ever been told about the past — it is all one story. Everyone who ever kept a fire was writing the same ledger."

A-Shi lowered his head.

"Then let me keep it," he said.

She nodded slowly.

"Light it, then."

A-Shi walked toward the third lamp.

The sun sank below the ridge. Night came down without warning.

Far off — so faint at first that he might have imagined it — a point of light pulsed in the dark — one, two, three.

The woman came out behind him, holding a burning bundle of straw like a candle against the night.

She let the flame fall into the old lamp's basin.

The third lamp lit — and burned, and burned, and burned.

Its light rose higher than anyone had ever seen a lamp burn, and it flooded the entire northern flatland. A-Shi stood before it with tears in his eyes.

Far ahead, farther than either of them could see, the line of lamps went on — north, all the way to the end of the world — every single one now alight — six hundred years finally waking after all those centuries of silence.

Three lamps burning, connected to the one in the south, connected back by smoke and wind and memory.

He looked down at the palm in his pocket, where the little black stone rested — still warm.

"Six hundred years," A-Shi said.

"Six hundred years," said the woman.

They stood together before the third lamp until dawn.
