## Sixty-one: Splitting the Firewood

After the spring came the season for splitting wood.

A-Shi carried his axe up the mountain.

He had been watching his mother for years, and for weeks he had watched the axe itself. He went and pulled down a dry pine branch onto the flat surface of a large stone, spread his legs apart, raised the axe above his head, and brought it down hard. The blade glanced off the wood and the handle vibrated in his hands.

He tried again from a different angle.

The ax did not go in. It skittered along the grain and stopped.

A-Shi stayed very still.

His mother had come up the path. She saw him sweating, his arms trembling.

"I'll help you," she said.

He shook his head.

He remembered her once telling him two things:

"Your feet planted wide."
"Your waist moves first. The axe falls second."

He stood straight. He spread his legs. He turned his torso slowly, held the ax loosely in both hands, and let the axe fall the way an autumn leaf falls — no force, just gravity.

The blade buried itself in the bark.

A loud ripping sound followed. The piece of pine split in two, clean halves, both sides falling apart exactly where they should.

A-Shi's eyes widened. He did not speak. He let go of the handle and just stood, watching the wind move over the mountainside.

His mother touched his shoulder lightly.

"Your grandfather split wood the same way," she said.

"I understand now," A-Shi said.

"Six hundred years?"

His mother smiled.

"That is how they taught him," she said, "and how his father before him, and all the way back."

A-Shi gathered the two halves of pine. The pain in his back was gone.

"Let's go home," he said.


He carried the wood down the slope.

His mother lit the stove when they returned.

The fire burned magnificently.

All afternoon and evening, A-Shi split more wood. Every log he handled, he managed.

The fireplace never wanted for anything until the snow was long gone and spring had fully arrived.
