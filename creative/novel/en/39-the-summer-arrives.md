## Thirty-nine: The Summer Arrives

Half of July had passed; the fields were turning gold.

Lin Wen sat in the courtyard mending a bamboo chair — one bow had snapped, and he had pulled it out and replaced it with a fresh cutting. His mother was preparing peas in the porch; the knife was moving slowly, like someone telling a very old story.

He almost asked: "Mother — is there one more page of that ledger you haven't shown me yet?"

She did not look up. "Your father told me once," she said. "*Where the fire stays, the home stays.*"

"I'm here," Lin Wen said.

His mother smiled. "I know."

That night a wind came through. The old persimmon tree let go of one fruit — no sound at all, and yet heavy where it struck the stone — landing just in front of the gate Lin Wen had not crossed when he left. One of the two remaining. The other still hangs.

Lin Wen did not pick it up. He reached over and took his mother's hand — her nails faintly dusty with pea skins — and held it the way a son would hold his own finger.

The next morning, the fruit lying in the courtyard had not been touched.

The neighbour's boy came past the gate on his way to the hills. He craned his neck over the wall, a stick in his hand longer than his own arm. He saw the two red persimmons on the branch and called out:

"Uncle — is that tree selling wine?"

Lin Wen knelt down to look at him. He felt an answer arriving in his chest — some clarity he had been carrying without knowing it.

"It isn't selling wine," he said. "It's carrying persimmons."

The boy lowered the stick, staring at the tree. The wind passed through its leaves in a soft, dry rustle.

"Then... may I eat one?"

Lin Wen reached into his jacket, felt the iron fire-token his fingers had memorised all these weeks, and held it where the boy could see.

He remembered the day his father had explained what it was. *Every generation of keeper leaves one behind.*

The boy reached out and gently touched the token with one finger.

"Then," Lin Wen said, "you keep it."

The boy looked at him — eyes catching the light on the remaining fruit the same way the fruit had caught it earlier in the day. He nodded.

Lin Wen stood up and placed a hand on the boy's shoulder. He stepped back half a step.

— and was surprised by what he saw: the child and the old tree, one small boy standing beneath the last two persimmons of the season, a long stick at his side, the whole yard still standing in the slant of late afternoon.

Halfway up the western ridge, a thin column of smoke was rising. Not cooking smoke — it was something from the Ember Post. He could not tell why it would be rising now, but it was.

Now he understood.

Far away, something had started to close the story he was part of.

— but the fire does not unravel.

— what remains, remains in this house.
