## Fifty-six: The Match

One night in early spring, A-Shi woke to find the candle in the clay bowl gone out.

The room was black. He sat for a long time without moving, without speaking.

The bowl had been dark; the iron fire-token he kept in his pillow box could not be found; every match had every corner. His own coat pockets, his pillowcase, his shoe soles — all empty.

He hesitated in the doorway; then he went to the small wooden chest his grandfather had brought back from the way-station and opened it. Bare.

And then — wedged between the last two panels of wood — one small strip of paper. A deliberate, fold of paper.

He tore it carefully open.

**Fire does not die — it sleeps.**

He sat with the paper a long time.

Then he got up.

He found his cloak, his boots, and he ran—not stopping until he reached the old yurt house on the hillside, where Du the Elder kept his stove.

Du was sitting in the doorway when he arrived. He had been expecting him.

A-Shi said nothing. He reached into his coat and held out the empty matchbox.

Du looked at it for a long moment. Then he looked at A-Shi's face.

"How long have you been keeping this?" he asked.

"My grandfather brought it back from the Yáohuǒ Station," A-Shi said.

"Where is he?"

A-Shi took a breath.

"Dead."

Du nodded. He did not nod in a shocked way — rather it was his quiet authority, the same look he gives everyone who comes through his gate.

"Come in," he said.

A-Shi stepped through the door.

Du lit a small stick, handed it to him, and started to prepare tea. Neither of them spoke. The water heated; the room warmed gradually.

At last Du said:

"Does your family have any fire left?"

"No," A-Shi said.

Du nodded again, slowly.

"Then we tend the altar here first."

He led A-Shi to a small stove at the back of the yurt, and lit it very carefully.

"Your grandfather came here," Du said. "He was weak. I looked after him."

A-Shi held the hot stick in his fingers. He didn't burn himself.

Du said: "Everyone carries things across the bridge — whether or not they know it. They don't know, but they see."

They stayed in silence for they both needed.

Before dawn, Du gave A-Shi one single match.

"Only one," he said. "That is what you take with you."

A-Shi took it. He did not ask why.

By morning, Du's yurt had filled with light from the stove. A-Shi ate porridge. Du watched him carefully.

Then A-Shi walked home.

He put the match inside the clay bowl at his house. The bowl did not light at once.

He knew better to wait. He sat beside the bowl a long time.

Eventually, he heard the faintest crack — the match in the bowl woke a new fire. It spread slowly into the wick. The candle burned again.

He did not cry. He only listened.

But later — when he was absolutely sure the bowl was lit and would not go out — he whispered so quietly:

"Grandpa, you are still here."

He stayed until dawn.

By morning, the whole valley had heard. From the valley ridge, from the south, smoke rose from every house — as if there had never been any question about the fire's survival.

What had gone out had only been resting, waiting for someone to bring the light back.
