## Two Hundred and Ninety-one: Embers

The morning after the festival, A-Shi woke before the sky had fully lightened.

There was a sweeping sound outside. Not the wind — bamboo branches over wet flagstones, one stroke, then another, with a breath between. He pulled on his clothes and went out. His mother was sweeping the courtyard. Last night's lanterns lay everywhere: the paper ones torn, their bamboo frames still whole; one tilted against the wall, its face marked with a small round scorch where a spark had landed.

A-Shi crouched to pick it up. The paper lantern was as light as a dry leaf in his hand, the edge of the burn hole curled, still carrying a faint smell of saltpetre. He gathered the bamboo frames into one pile, the paper into another. His mother did not turn around. "Keep the bamboo," she said. "We can paste new paper on it next year."

In the old clay jar by the corner lay the spent shell of a firework the children had set off, its red paper curling like a burnt lip. A-Shi nudged it into the ash with a stick and covered it with earth. The ash was still warm. He remembered the light at midnight — the whole lane bright for one instant, the ridge itself drawn in outline — and now there was only this cold jar of grey.

Something hung on the door latch. He leaned closer: a little wooden bird, half its tail missing, one eye painted with a dot of charcoal. He did not recognise it. Some child must have dropped it in the rush last night and hung it there to wait for morning. He set it on the windowsill and did not take it inside.

The stove gave a small sound from within the house.

He went in and added two sticks of wood. The flames licked up, whitening the ends of the sticks before swallowing them slowly. Half a pot of yesterday's soup remained; he ladled a bowl and stood by the stove drinking it. The soup was cold, the fat set in a pale layer. His mother came in, took the bowl to warm it, and put it back in his hand. "The festival is over," she said. "The fire isn't."

A-Shi looked into the firebox. Beneath the flames lay a few dark-red coals, not brilliant, but not out all night. He understood suddenly that last night's fireworks and this stove-fire were not the same thing: fireworks were for the sky, spent in an instant; the stove-fire was for the days, burning night after night.

In the courtyard the fire-seed tree still had a torn ribbon caught in a branch, heavy with dew. A-Shi went over, untied it, dried it by the stove, folded it, and laid it on the windowsill beside the wooden bird.

The charcoal dot of the bird's eye, touched by the firelight, seemed still to shine.

From behind him his mother said, "Someone will come for that bird."

"If nobody does, it stays," A-Shi said.

The fire burned.

The fire burned. Last night's ash, today's wood, tomorrow's seed — all in this one stove, and never once broken.
