# The Unchanted Chronicle of Crossrealm: The Keeper of the Anchor Fire

## Seventy-nine: Into the Outlands

Five hungry days after the third spark-week, the stove-fire fell still.

Nothing obvious changed in the hearth — the same low glow rested against the embers, the same fire lit the same corners of the kitchen. But each morning since, when A-Shi added wood before dawn, something subtle pulled at him from inside the stove itself — a weight so faint it might have been a heartbeat, except that it kept its own rhythm, not his.

Baiyin came once a month. She had never once announced what she was going to say — but he knew, now, that everything she had ever said had always, always been the same thing.

On the sixth month of this stillness, she stood at the threshold and said:

"You must leave the mountain."

"Where?" A-Shi asked.

Baiyin held up her hand and said one word:

"North."

"North?"

Baiyin reached into her sleeve and produced a small fire-sickle — a curved steel strip with a dark, half-burned pine-knot for a handle. She set it on the kitchen table with the same precise care as a woman setting a spell.

"Six hundred years ago," Baiyin said, "when the first coal was lit at this family's stove, the fire-keepers of the valley all lit their own keepers' fires — and from there, beyond the ridge, other keepers took it, and kept it, from there onward."

She lifted one finger toward the ridge beyond the village.

"The ridge has three more lamps — there, there, and there — all marked by the same six-hundred-year rule, same keepers, same silence."

A-Shi stared at the ridge.

"They have burned for six hundred years too?"

"They have burned for six hundred years too," Baiyin said.

She fixed her eyes on A-Shi.

"You may carry a jar of this stove's ash up there, to show them what you have kept — or you may walk up there just to see."

"Who will mind the stove?" A-Shi asked. "Who will mind my mother?"

Baiyin smiled faintly.

"Seven days," she said. "Everything will be in place in seven days."

She paused, turned one last time, and added — with her voice lower than before:

"The hill keeper tends the stove. The outlands keeper tends the fire. They are two different kinds of tending."

Then she walked out the door and was gone, her sandals whispering against the frozen earth.

Before she disappeared over the rise, A-Shi saw one more thing — the little empty wooden box Baiyin always carried at her waist. She was wearing it today, too.

"What is that box for?" he called after her.

Baiyin did not turn.

"You will find out," came the answer, carried back to him on the cold wind.

A-Shi stood at the stove for a long time afterward. He could feel the stove working the way it always had — the embers pulsing quietly under the new wood, the ash settling into patterns only he knew how to read.

Six hundred years of keeper work.

The stove didn't care whether he stayed or went. It cared only that someone tended it — and it remembered.

He thought: what keeps this fire alive was never that one person stayed at this stove. What keeps this fire alive is the chain, the long unbroken chain.

Six hundred years, and now the chain had loosened — and a link had shifted outward, into the mountain.

He went into the cellar. From the lowest bin he lifted the iron canister holding the six-hundred-year-old coal — the first coal ever kindled at this stove, the black coal that had been sleeping in the heart of the white stone, that lightning had let fall into his hand.

He held it for a moment. He placed it gently back inside the canister. He did not take it.

Seven days later, A-Shi laid out grandfather's traveling bag. He laid the pine needles, the ledger-page fragment, and a single sprig of the fire-seed tree's red needles on the kitchen table. His mother came out to dry the rice grain and stopped dead at the sight of them — but she didn't ask.

A-Shi asked to borrow line-ink, coarse paper, and a stopper of stove-ash. His mother put them into his hands without a word.

At dawn on the seventh day, A-Shi kneaded a fresh bundle, said goodbye to his mother, shouldered the traveling bag, and stepped through the gate.

The wind was tremendous — tremendous, enormous, as if the entire mountain had been waiting for him to walk into it.

He looked east. The ridge stood in the low sunlight — the crack in the white stone glinted with a reddish warmth, unearthly and blessed.

No one called after him. No one asked him to stay. The stove simply burned on behind him.

He closed his eyes. He bowed toward the stove, toward the kitchen, toward the roof that had sheltered this house six hundred years.

"I'll come back soon," he said.

The stove gave one small, bright leap — a snap of flame inside, like a heartbeat answering his voice.

Then A-Shi set off, eastward, toward the mountain, toward the ridge, toward the three nameless lamps beyond it.
