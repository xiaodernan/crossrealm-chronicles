## Forty-three: A Full Night

On one winter Saturday, A-Shi stayed alone to guard the bowl at the gate.

His grandmother had gone to her sister's house, and Lin Wen had travelled north with his own mother for the new year. A-Shi remained in the old house, a small stool pulled up beside the clay bowl, a thick blanket over his knees, the candle standing straight in its dish — shorter than the night before, but alive.

Then he noticed the flame was leaning.

He shot up. He held a match in his palm and started to reach for the candle — but he stopped. *Fire must not be blown.* He hadn't decided which old rule to follow.

A passerby pushed open the gate.

Wind poured in under the eave. The candle went out. The dish went dark.

A-Shi did not move. He could not bring himself to call out. He sat stunned until the silence had wholly settled.

He drifted to the pile of dry branches by the gate, and sat there a long time. The moon was half-lit and frozen in a ring of thin silver.

He thought — one other time he had lost the fire, back in the early winter — how Lin Wen had pulled him up, given him a match, and he had refused to strike it.

"Failing the fire once isn't the disaster," Lin Wen had said, patient and entirely clear. "The disaster would be never wanting to strike again."

A-Shi stood up. He went back to the bowl.

He took the box of matches out of the smaller pot where it had been hidden since the house narrowed for the season. Only one match remained.

He struck it. No flame.

Second strike. Nothing.

Third strike. The tip gave but refused to catch.

His hands were ice-cold and too small for anything. He crouched down. He sang, or he perhaps whispered a name nobody heard. Fourth strike — he shielded the flicker with a curved notch of dried persimmon leaf — and the wick caught.

Light flooded the dish. Cold wax poured slowly now into the puddle, pale and trembling, the color of ice that remembers it once was water.

A-Shi knelt without crying.

He took the pen stub from his pocket, smoothed the back of an old letter, and wrote one line.

**Fire is still here.**

He pressed the paper on the dish and went to sleep.

In the morning Lin Wen returned. He read the note for a long moment. He didn't ask. He cooked rice, and set the bowl in front of A-Shi.

"Eat," Lin Wen said.

A-Shi looked up.

Lin Wen said: "You stayed all night."

A-Shi didn't answer.

"When did you learn?"

A-Shi spoke low: "I couldn't learn. But I couldn't let go."

Lin Wen smiled.

That year, the old persimmon tree let go of its last leaf. The wind brought it into the dish. The fire stayed lit.

A-Shi never recoiled from a failure again — every night he took charge of tending, he added a small persimmon leaf to the bowl before fanning it.

He learned to keep watch without screaming for help.

— he learned to strike again.

— that is all a keeper ever needs to know.
