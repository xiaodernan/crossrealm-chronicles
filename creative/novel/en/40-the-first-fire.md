## Forty: The First Fire

After the end of July, A-Shi came by every day.

He never walked in at once. Each time he called out from the gate first — "Uncle" — and only stepped inside when Lin Wen answered. His mother, up on the porch preparing supper, said nothing and pretended not to notice.

A-Shi crouched by the sapling frame, watching the red cord, watching the soil, watching the fire.

The first time he ever spoke, he asked:

"What's this red rope called?"

"Red cord," Lin Wen said. "Your grandma tied it."

A-Shi blinked. "I don't have a grandma anymore."

"Then it's yours," Lin Wen said.

A-Shi did not understand, but he nodded. He bent down and touched the cord. The sun had worn it pale.

One afternoon he did not come. Three days passed.

On the fourth day he arrived carrying an old cloth sack. Inside were a bundle of dried jujube leaves and half of a broken jujube branch — his own yard's old jujube had withered, and the leaves had been gathered cleanly.

"Uncle," he said, spreading the leaves on the ground, "my family's old jujube tree died too."

Lin Wen said nothing.

"Do I still get to keep the fire, Uncle?" A-Shi asked.

Lin Wen poured him a bowl of water and sat silent for a time.

"Fire can always be kept, A-Shi."

"But when there is nothing left — what do we keep it with?"

Lin Wen extended a finger to point at the soil beneath the sapling frame.

"Look here."

A-Shi crouched. Out of the packed soil, a small shoot pushed up — two leaves, pale green, thin enough to let the light pass through.

"That's the seedling's seed," Lin Wen said. "It is a persimmon."

A-Shi's eyes went wide.

"Every seed inside every fruit bears a tree," Lin Wen said. "When one persimmon falls, two rise in its place."

A-Shi said nothing, but he remembered the sentence. *A seed that fell would rise in its place.*

That night, when the fire was freshly re-fed, Lin Wen noticed the boy was still in the courtyard. A-Shi had fallen asleep beneath the persimmon tree with the iron fire-token in both hands, its outline worn smooth by his fingers. Before dawn he opened his eyes and found Lin Wen standing on the porch.

"Uncle," he asked, "does every tree carry inside it a spring no one has seen yet?"

Lin Wen said: "Yes. But spring has to be lit by someone."

A-Shi nodded.

The next day he did not come.

The third morning, Lin Wen pushed open the gate and found A-Shi sweeping the fallen persimmon leaves in the corner. He had spread them one by one on an old cloth.

"I'm taking them home, Uncle," he said. "If my jujube tree ever grows a bud — I'll light the leaves to meet it."

Lin Wen lifted the boy. He was so light — seven or eight, no more — and set him on the threshold. He looked at him for a long time.

"A-Shi," Lin Wen said, "do you want to learn how to keep the fire?"

A-Shi laughed for a long time before he finally said:

"Then... when I walk away, can I take the fire with me?"

Lin Wen shook his head.

"The fire never leaves," he said.

A-Shi nodded, his voice low. "Then I'll... stay."

A single leaf fell and landed in his lap. He looked at it for a long while — a small, half-translated letter.

Another persimmon fell. The second. The threshold stone has absorbed a hollow where the fruit rests each season. The fire in the stove was as steady as a hand.

That night A-Shi did not leave. He sat beside the sapling frame until the moon was very high, and finally at half past three he walked home.

Lin Wen's mother stood on the porch with the red cord in her hands and touched the frame gently. She said, barely audibly:

"The seed my mother left. The people I left with Lin Wen. The people Lin Wen keeps with A-Shi."

Lin Wen sat and breathed very calmly. He knew, at last, that he had received the next generation.
