## Twenty-three: The Moon Rises

Having sat a long time under the persimmon tree, Lin Wen heard a quiet *tck* from behind the house.

One note, just once. The furnace does not ring bells and does not walk roads — the furnace remembers the second a match is struck against the stone, or the moment a lamp first sees the dark it was lit against.

He closed his eyes and listened much longer. The sound was not "the fire has gone out." It was "the fire has finally been lit." It resembled something Lin Wen recalled from another lifetime of this — the very second he opened this book.

He opened his eyes again. The moon had risen.

The path behind the house had been swept with moonlight into a silver road — not for him, but for every reader still trying to remember why he came out of his own door. Lin Wen set himself down under the eaves and watched the moon climb one hill after another. He had understood for a long time: the reason he read to this page was not because he wanted to finish the book. It was because reading every page was the thing that had made him him.

No one else stood under the persimmon tree, only Lin Wen himself. The moonlight dragged his shadow long across the flagstones to the wall. He pushed to his feet, gathered a handful of dry kindling, and deliberately added it to the fire.

The fire went *tck* again — not the sound of dry wood catching, but of the fire itself consenting.

After the second *tck* the moonlight reached the lip of the hearth and landed upon a small slab of stone. On the slab, there was a single red book.

The cover was red — precisely the shade of the sash his mother had bound around his waist at the village gate on the first morning he left home.

He did not reach out. He stood there for a long time, watching the book rest untouched as though it had never moved. He understood that, even if he opened it, inside it would only be the one line he himself had not yet written — a line that had remained unwritten precisely because this page had been left for the one who had truly arrived.

He walked over, picked up the book, and flipped it over. On the back, written in a very small handwriting in his mother's hand, was a single line:

*Do not close the book. Close the night. Take tonight as a gift that was meant only for you.*

He slipped the book into his coat and sat back down.

The moon rose over many another hill. The shadow of the persimmon tree swept a full circle — and the moonlight tracked a silver arc across the path, ending at the window, resting on the page.

The book had opened itself. No one touched it.

Lin Wen read for a long time, then walked back under the persimmon tree.

The moon washed everything in a soft silver: the silhouette, the shadow, the still-verses in the book. The persimmon leaves fell, lifted, and fell again — he stopped counting.

Tonight, no one else was awake. Tonight, only these words — and the one reading them.
