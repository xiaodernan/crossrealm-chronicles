## Eighty-five: The Letter

Three days after the Solstice, A-Shi decided to write to Baiyin.

He reached under the stove ledge for a square of trimmed kitchen paper — just the right size — and laid it flat on the table. The fire-light gilded it orange. He set down the brush.

The stroke began — a single short horizontal, heavy and brief, as if stamped out of his grandfather's hand.

He stared at it.

His grandfather's stroke, yes. His father's vertical, yes. His own ink — and it was also his grandfather's ink, mixed from the same stove ash.

He crossed out the横.

He crossed out the竖.

He thought — about everything he wanted to say: about the ridge, the ledger, his mother's silence, the three days he had not slept.

He crossed out everything he wanted to say.

He picked up the brush once more — this time, his own stroke.

His was lighter than his father's, faster than his grandfather's.

He wrote one line:

The fire will not go out.

Nothing else.

Not a word more.

He folded the paper into quarters, then folded it again until it was thin enough to tuck into the chest-bag — the way Baiyin always did her own letters. He slipped it under the stove's ledge, where his grandfather's book was kept — where the ledger lived — where his own pulse had somehow started leaping in time with the stove.

His mother, sweeping the floor, picked up the folded square. She looked at it — then at him — said nothing. She set it aside, beside the ledger.

That night the stove leapt once, sharply.

A-Shi looked up.

The ledger opened itself.

On the final page, in real ink — not copied, not simulated, but truly his grandfather's hand — letters appeared that have not been there before:

A sealed book, and it wakes itself.

He did not touch it.

He waited.

The next morning the page was blank again — except one line:

I will be the one to see it inside the ledger.

Baiyin's brush.

A-Shi looked at the stove.

He set the folded letter down on the stove's hottest corner, closed his hand tight around it, and took a long breath.

Sealed.

Sealed for good.

Six hundred years and the flames still rose.
