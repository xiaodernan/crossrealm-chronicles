## Sixty-seven: The Kindle

In the winter of the year that A-Shi turned eighteen, his mother did not send him to the woodpile as she always had. She set two bowls on the stove ledge and sat down, her voice measured in that quiet way that meant important things were coming.

"A thing for you to do tomorrow morning," she said.

"What?"

"Kindle."

A-Shi lowered his bowl. He thought he had misheard her.

"What is kindle?"

She laughed first, then grew serious again.

"Our house has kept fire since six hundred years ago. Before every new keeper takes charge of the hearth alone, they walk out and carry small sparks of other fires back home. The fire in this stove didn't light itself — six hundred years ago, someone laid the first stick. That first flame passed to the hearth, then to the beacon on the ridge, then to every lamp in every house. Kindle means learning that the fire you keep was raised, little by little, by the whole valley."

She reached into her collar and drew out an old cloth bundle. Inside it sat a clay jar no bigger than a thumb — the lid sealed with candle wax dark with age.

"Your great-great-grandfather's kindle-jar. Six hundred years, seven keepers have used it. The jar is still the same."

A-Shi took it carefully. The bundle was heavier than it looked.

"How do I kindle?" he asked.

"Start from our hearth. Then to Uncle Ye's house at the east end of the lane, the ever-burning lamp at the crossroads, Aunty Li's incense pot, and last of all the night-lamp up on the white stone on the ridge. At each place, gather a small ember into your jar. No fire-steel, no firestone — you may only light your own spark from someone else's flame. Once you have all five, come home and we'll join every one of them into the stove."

One more thing, she added:

"Leave the wax alone. It was sealed in the six-hundredth year."

A-Shi slept little, and fitfully.

At first light he carried his grandfather's old kindle-jar out of the village. The winter wind was cruelly sharp. The east end of the lane, he found Uncle Ye sweeping snow off his doorstep. Ye saw the jar and stopped.

"Kindle?"

A-Shi nodded.

Uncle Ye tapped out a little coal from his pipe bowl, blew on it twice and said: "You're from the A-Shi house. Can't say the words enough. This fire — I'll hold it for you."

Then he reached into his stove and drawn out a pinch of living red coal, wrapped it in paper, and placed it in A-Shi's open palm. It was not hot enough to burn him — just warm, soaking deep into the small of his hand.

"Six hundred years," Uncle Ye said. "The fire your family warmed this village with — it's in my stove too. Carry it home."

The crossroads lamp was tended by the temple. The old Daoist said not one word; he simply lowered the lamp wick lower so A-Shi could dip his ember in. Everything was as it should be. At Aunty Li's, she was in middle of her morning prayers; when she heard the request, she lit three incense sticks on the spot and pressed the head of one against the mouth of his jar — incense, hearth, ever-burning lamp, pipe coal — four fires lay quiet in the jar.

One more place. The white stone on the ridge.

When he climbed up, the snow had stopped. Baiyin was gone. Only a lamp remained — the same one he had lit there on that autumn night half a year ago, its flame barely visible, steady as ever. Snow lay thick across the glass shell, not one spark escaped.

A-Shi knelt by the stone a while. He leaned the jar up against the lamp's mouth and cupped his hand over them both —

The wick bent, softly, like it remembered him. Or like it could not bear to leave. From the jar came a faint crackling, the same sound falling embers make when they tumble into ash.

He carried the jar down the mountain cradled against his chest.

By the time he returned, his mother was waiting at the stove. Two small stools were near the hearth. She carefully opened the jar. The wax seal was intact, perfect down to the last line.

She did not ask how the fire had gone. She only said one word:

"Join them."

A-Shi fed six hundred years — he'd never before felt it so vividly — into the stove, little by little, from the jar. The fire made no sound and no smoke; it only steadied into a darker, heavier glow, and then began to brighten. Brighter than before — like the last sliver of sun on the river at dusk, but steady, and gentle, and warm.

"The stove stands again," his mother said.

"And what now?" A-Shi asked.

"Now you'll understand that fire never waits for anyone," she said. "You answer for these five fires, because you have already lived their six hundred years for them."

He laid his hand on the stove ledge anyway, feeling the stove's deep, even heat. Some part of him had finally opened —

Keepers of six hundred years are not born from the stove alone; they're kindled, flame by flame, from every lamp in the village, every incense pot, every pipe coal, every ember that refuses to go out. They are one fire, many children, scattered across six hundred years, now come home at last.

He set a stick on the stove's shoulder, and in the ash the spark fluttered twice.

"Put the jar away," his mother said. "Keep it for your own child."

He nodded.

That night, he sealed the jar with wax again and placed it back in the wooden box. He thought of Baiyin waiting alone on the white stone. He thought of the six hundred years of keepers who had walked the same path, step by step. He thought of tomorrow — what he must do.

To keep fire, he thought, one must be like fire itself.

Unquenchable.
