## Two Hundred and Ninety-two: The Bird

Someone came for the bird in the afternoon.

An old man, down from the far side of the ridge, his cloth shoes grey with dust that was not snow but the alkali frost that never melts on the high ground. He stopped at the doorway, did not come in, and looked for a long time at the windowsill. A-Shi saw his gaze settle on the little wooden bird, on the charcoal dot of its eye, and stay there.

"I carved it," the old man said.

His mother came out of the house, still holding the greens she had been trimming. She knew the face — every year around the start of spring this man came down from the ridge, sat at the village entrance for half an hour without a word, glanced once at the fire-seed tree in their yard, and left. This year he had walked further, all the way to their door.

"Lost it three days ago," the old man said. "During the festival I set it on your latch, meaning to take it at first light. I didn't mean to lose it."

A-Shi took the bird down from the sill and held it out. The old man did not take it at once — first he wiped it with his sleeve, brushing off the sill-dust and the one smudge of stove smoke. Only then did he cup it in both hands, turn it over, and run a thumb along the broken half of its tail.

"It was the seventh I carved," he said. "The first six stayed on the mountain. I meant to carry this one home, but it spent the night travelling with your festival lanterns instead."

Wind poured through the gate and the fire-seed tree's needles rustled in a sheet. The old man looked up at the tree and looked for a long while, as though counting the new layer it had put on this year.

"It's taller," he said.

"It is," his mother said.

"Seven hundred years," the old man said. "My father's father's father, when he was small, saw someone bury the first seed under this tree. It wasn't higher than his knee then."

A-Shi's hand stopped on the axe he had been holding. He had never heard his mother speak of anything buried beneath the tree.

The old man seemed to read his thought and smiled. "No treasure. Just a burnt coal. When a person dies, the fire must not die with them, so they bury one coal under the tree. Year after year. There's no telling how many your tree is standing on."

The stove gave another small sound from inside, as if answering.

His mother let the old man into the yard and brought him a bowl of hot water. He held it without drinking and asked first: "That stove — has it never gone out?"

"Never," his mother said.

"Good," the old man said. "They want to know, on the far side. I'll tell them: it never went out."

He drank once, set the bowl down, and drew from his coat something wrapped in birch bark. He laid it on the corner of the table. A-Shi saw characters written on the bark in charcoal — not the village hand, the strokes harder, as though cut into wood.

"Not a gift," the old man said. "A reply. The line you asked me to carry up the mountain last year — 'the stove fire is still bright' — the far side sent a line back. I'm late bringing it. It reaches you today."

He rose, cupping the wooden bird, and moved toward the door. At the threshold he stopped and turned back to A-Shi:

"I'm taking the bird. Don't mend the broken tail. It spent a festival night with you; it should come home looking like that."

A-Shi nodded.

The old man walked into the white afternoon light, his cloth shoes crossing the stone step, leaving grey prints that the wind thinned and took away. He did not look back.

A-Shi picked up the birch bark from the table corner and turned it over. On the back were four characters. He knew two of them — "fire" and "still" — and did not know the other two. His mother came over, glanced at it, and read them aloud: "The fire endures."

"What does 'endures' mean?"

"It means it doesn't go out," his mother said. "A longer name for it than 'still bright.'"

A-Shi turned the bark over and over, then set it on the windowsill where the wooden bird had been. The stove-light reached out from inside and fell exactly on those charcoal characters.

The fire-seed tree's needles rustled again, as though someone very far away had answered, softly.

The fire burned.
