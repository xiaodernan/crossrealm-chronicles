## One Hundred and Seven: The Nameless Twig

Fifteen days after the old man had left, the first frost of the season fell on the courtyard.

The frost was white — nearly the same white as the white birch. A thin film of it hung from every needle of the fire-seed tree; when the wind stirred, it rained down onto the stone steps before the stove, a fine white dusting.

It was the morning A-Shi woke that he went out to split the firewood and saw something new beside the gate.

A bundle of firewood.

White birch branches — each one straight, each one dry to the core, each one exactly the width of a man's palm. They were tied with a strip of old cloth, the cloth knotted in a style he did not recognize. And tucked under the cloth was a small folded slip of paper.

On the paper, two characters:

添柴

"More fuel."

A-Shi carried the bundle back into the courtyard and passed the slip to his mother. She read it once, and her eyes were wet before she had finished.

She did not ask who it was.

"Last night there was a knock at the gate," she said. "I thought it was the wind."

The whole family stood in the courtyard and looked at the bundle.

Xiao Huo came loping out from beside the stove, bent down to peer at the paper, tried two characters he could not read, then picked up one of the birch twigs and ran back to the stove and fed it into the fire.

The stove leapt once.

It did not grow brighter.

It did not die.

It simply — accepted it, quietly.

A-Shi stacked the remaining six birch twigs beside the stove, each one in its own place. He said nothing. His mother said nothing.

That morning the bundle of nameless birch was set down at the place nearest the stove, where the next person to need fuel would see it first.

His mother ran a finger over the cloth strip.

The cloth was grey — not the white silk of Bai Yin's packet. It was patched in three places, always with small, square lines of beige thread.

A-Shi recognized that kind of stitching. His uncle had used it on the cuffs of his own work coat.

He said nothing.

That evening he took a bucket of water and a rag and washed down the stone tablet at the gate — the one with only the white birch branch carved into it. He poured water over it, then dried it with a cloth.

In the moonlight, the grain of the carved branch caught the light and returned it, faint and white — as though something were glowing from within the stone itself.

Seven hundred years, no one had ever fed their stove so much as a single twig.

That night, nobody in the house said a word.

His mother tucked another slip of paper beneath the iron box. On it, in her small and crooked hand, she wrote:

"Seven hundred years. The first nameless twig. The stove has seen it."

The next morning, one of the sticks on the stove's border was missing. Someone had fed it into the fire sometime in the night — and no one had seen it happen.

Seven nameless birch twigs.

His mother said: "A-Shi, count. Count them —"

He counted. "Seven," he said.

"Seven hundred years —" she began. And stopped.

Xiao Huo looked at her.

She did not finish the sentence.

Instead she said: "Come next spring, when the flowers open, if we see another bundle like this, then we will know — this house is no longer the only one keeping the fire."

That night the stove burned higher than it had ever burned.

No one asked it to.

It simply burned higher.
