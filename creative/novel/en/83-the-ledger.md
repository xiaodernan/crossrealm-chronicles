## Eighty-three: The Ledger

On the seventh morning after they came home, A-Shi woke alone.

His mother had gone out early. The kitchen held nothing but the stove and himself.

He took down the ledger from beside the stove.

The cover was thin — the kind of thing people throw away after a few years. Not much like a treasure. He opened it anyway.

On the first page a very faint character was written in ink gone pale with time:

记.

He turned the page. The next character was also 记. The third was 记. Every page — every single page — held the same character, written by a different hand, with a different temperament, in a different season of the writer's life.

He read three generations of hands.

His grandfather's stroke — short across, tall down, heavy on the finish. His father's stroke — lighter, smaller, more careful, as though conscious of taking up space. His great-great-grandfather's stroke — away on the corner — bold and unsteady.

He saw age in the ink long before he saw it in faces.

He turned page after page. His hands grew heavier with each one.

He reached the final, blank page.

He sat there for a long time.

He wanted to do something grand — to write the character with a flourish nobody could forget, to put six hundred years of ink into one gesture. Then he understood: nobody wanted that. The last keeper had not tried to out-do the others.

A ray of sunlight fell through the kitchen window and lit the center of the stove-fire.

He took the brush from the holder, set it in the inkstone, and wrote.

Three strokes. One breath. The same character.

The stroke for 记 (to remember) — a little wider on the right than it was on the left, tilting slightly into the habit he had practised a thousand times.

He set the brush down.

Three strokes. One breath.

He looked at what he had written.

记.

Horizontal stroke his grandfather's habit. Vertical his father's. Ink his own.

From the doorway behind him:

"You finally did it."

Baiyin.

He didn't ask when she had arrived.

"You see?" he said, without turning. "It is a little of all of them."

Baiyin came to his side and studied the character.

"Beautiful," she said.

He shook his head.

"I wrote it by hand. Wait until the village sees it."

Baiyin laughed.

"The village never will," she said. "This book is yours alone."

She held it for a moment, then handed it back.

"Tomorrow," she said, "you begin the next book."

"The next book?"

"The one they are all waiting for," Baiyin said.

He asked nothing more.

That evening the stove burned higher than it usually did, and the fire-seed tree rustled though there was no wind. He understood that the next volume of the ledger was already open — and that he would never be able to refuse the next step.

Some things are not inherited.

They are kindled.
