# The Unchanted Chronicle of Crossrealm: The Keeper of the Anchor Fire

## Seventy-six: Thunderfire

Halfway through June, the stove remained more than the household had ever had to cope with — and yet, on certain days, not enough.

A-Shi kept his habits: tending the fire, watering the seed-tree, practicing the character. The tree in the yard carried seven needles. The village said that summer would be quiet — even the cicadas sang less.

One evening, the clouds rolled in from the west.

Black as spilled ink on faded paper. The wind grew. A-Shi hurried to gather the washing before the storm could knock the house down — but as he was about to close the door, he looked up toward the ridge.

His pupils narrowed.

The white stone at the summit was struck by lightning.

A violet-white bolt split the night open. A heartbeat later, a thunderclap that threw every window into trembling. Even the stove flames shrank against their base.

A-Shi did not wait for the echo. He was already running for the mountain, heedless of rain.

"Into the rain?" his mother caught his sleeve. "The rain's foul —"

"The stove-fire," he said.

She let go of him.

He ran through the rain, uphill, arms scratched by scree and hail. At the ridge's midpoint he saw the white stone cracked wide open.

The night-lamp —

Gone.

The lamp-stand was still there; the glass was shattered to nothing. After six hundred years of undying flame, lightning had finished it in one stroke.

A-Shi knelt in the mud, the rain streaming down his face — though nothing could have washed away the fear in him. No insect had survived six hundred years. No wildfire. No drought. Not even an earthquake. Until — now.

Then he heard it — a gust of wind from the valley below, with the unmistakable trace of woodsmoke on it.

He followed it down the ridge, into the dark.

Another crack of thunder — the whole mountain shuddered.

He looked back — the stove-light flickered from the far east, a thin gleam that burned through the rain, as if something — hidden — was still burning.

East —

He remembered.

East of the village there was a stretch of barren flatland no one ever walked. On that day, six hundred years ago, his great-great-grandfather had given one of the first sparks to a traveller — the peddler who carried the fire to the ever-burning lamp. Beyond that, the peddler had planted a forest of pines on the flats — a forest no villager had ever entered.

The wind was coming from there.

A-Shi ran toward the light.

The wind was savage. The rain was a solid wall. He fought forward as though through smoke. At last he reached the forest's edge — and saw one single tree burn at its highest tip, a crown of steady blue flame.

The Lord of the Pines.

Before he could stand fully upright, his hand tipped — the one spoonful of ash-water still sloshing in the ladle, the last stove-ash of six hundred years, the only thing in the world that could prove him the keeper.

He poured the ash-water onto the blue flame.

The blue flame did not die. It turned gold.

Gold fire swept through the rain and the hail — and A-Shi's worn arm was burning, burning cold and hot at once.

He stood upright then, into the fire, facing it.

"Six hundred and first keeper!" he shouted into the wind. "I have come for what is yours — and what is ours!"

The flame-column wavered —

… then bent, gently, toward him — and settled — slow and steady — into the little wooden box in his chest, where the stove-seed tree's pine needle was still resting in a bolt of warm cloth.

The fire had come home.

A-Shi staggered backward out of the pine forest, clutching his chest with both hands.

The rain stopped.

Under the bruised white sky, every needle-tip in the pine flats glowed soft gold — one by one, line by line, connecting all the way down the mountain toward home.

He raised his head and saw — his mother standing on the ridge's shoulder, waiting.

"The summit was destroyed," she said.

"The stove-fire still burns," A-Shi said.

His mother froze.

She turned and walked down to the stove. In the hearth, the fire was still burning — brighter than ever — the flame long and steady and clear, as if something had chosen, once more, to carry it forward.

In the yard, the fire-seed tree shimmered — its newest needles trembling in a wind no one else could feel.

All night long.
