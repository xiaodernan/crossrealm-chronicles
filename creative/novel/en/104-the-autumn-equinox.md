## One Hundred and Four: The Autumn Equinox

On the day of the autumn equinox, the fog inside the courtyard burned off earlier than it ever had before.

When A-Shi's mother woke before dawn, the stove was cold. A single ember of the previous night cowered in the firebox, low and patient, like a sleeping animal.

She went to the stove and crouched for a long time, peering into the firebox, saying nothing at all.

Xiao Huo was still asleep in the back room. A-Shi had risen first and gone out to the outer yard to split wood.

His mother did not wake Xiao Huo. She kindled the fire herself. When the first flame climbed, she heard the even thud of the ax against birch from outside — thud, thud, thud — every stroke measured, never wasted.

She brought out the remaining piece of white birch.

She looked at it for a long time.

Then she cut it into three — a large piece, a smaller one, and a tiny splinter. The tiny splinter she fed to the new fire. The other two she wrapped in cloth and laid under the iron box, beside the letter Bai Yin had written six hundred years before.

One piece per fire, Bai Yin had noted in her hand: that was all the white birch was ever good for.

The split birch lay beside the stove, pale as frost. His mother fed the first good piece to the fire. The blaze leapt once, as if it had recognized something, then settled into a deep, steady glow.

Xiao Huo woke soon after.

He ran barefoot to the stove and saw the fresh white birch burning. He ran to the threshold and saw A-Shi standing at the stove with tears in his eyes — no one said anything, but the flame burned brighter and cleaner than it ever had.

A-Shi finally spoke, his voice barely above a whisper:

"Do you see?"

Xiao Huo shook his head.

"The stove fed itself," A-Shi said, and he pointed into the firebox. "We didn't do it. It — fed itself."

Xiao Huo looked into the firebox again.

Inside, unmistakably, there was a piece of white birch that nobody had set there.

That night his mother kept vigil while Xiao Huo slept, the way Bai Yin's line had kept it for seven hundred years.

The next morning, the three of them climbed the mountain — A-Shi, his mother, and Xiao Huo, carried sometimes on A-Shi's back, sometimes scrambling along the narrow path in short, breathless dashes.

The path was steep, but it was not long. Xiao Huo had never walked so far. At half-height he stopped and squatted down, too winded to take another step until A-Shi lifted him onto his back.

His mother said nothing. She did not stop him either.

At the very top of the white stone ridge stood a small stone house. The roof was flat — no tiles, just one wide polished slab of pale rock facing the sun.

In front of the stone house stood an old man.

He wore the same bleached hemp robe A-Shi had seen before, the cuffs gone white with age and washing — the very man who had delivered the letter. He was older now, white-faced as the rock behind him.

When he saw A-Shi's mother carrying Xiao Huo, and A-Shi with wild mushrooms collected from the northern slope, he knelt on one knee.

So did his mother.

For the first time in seven hundred years, the two keepers — the keeper of the stove below and the keeper of the ancestor-flame above — knelt facing one another across the ridge in silence.

Neither of them spoke.

The wind moved over the white stone. On the far side of the ridge, the ancestor-flame burned in its own single hearth beyond the stone, and the stone itself bent the light back, so that the faces of all three were brightened in the same white-gold glow.

His mother opened her bundle and drew out a small bag — a bag of ash from their own stove.

She held it out.

The old man received it. He reached into his own robe and drew out a small porcelain jar — inside it, a pinch of ash from the ancestor-flame.

He tipped the ancestor-ash into the little bag of stove-ash, and then tipped a little of the stove-ash back into his porcelain jar.

The two colors could not be told apart by the naked eye.

They climbed back down the mountain side by side.

Xiao Huo slept on A-Shi's back.

He dreamed of a girl named Bai standing on a ridge, watching the ancestor-flame above her and the stove-flame below her, and the two flames saw one another across six hundred years and seven hundred years, blind but answering.

When he woke, they were home.

He was sleeping at the foot of the stove. The fire lit his face in gold.

The fire had climbed one inch higher than it had been the night before.
