## Forty-one: The Tending

The night Lin Wen asked A-Shi to stay and watch the stove fire, his mother had gone to visit a distant cousin. As she left, she said one thing:

"What he is keeping tonight — is this one fire."

A-Shi nodded. When the yard grew dark, he carried a low stool to the hearth, set the candle in a plain white dish with a stainless rim, and settled himself beside it. The clay bowl stood at the gate, upright and unmoving.

At about one in the morning he woke. The candle had dwindled to a thread — thin, tremulous, and about to break. His whole body stiffened. He wanted to blow it up the flame — but something in this house had always told him: fire must never be blown. He did not blow.

The candle went out.

A-Shi did not dare to move. He looked at the dish for a long time, at the cold pool of wax, at the little stub of a wick lying in the puddle. He didn't call Lin Wen. He didn't speak at all.

The next morning, when Lin Wen went to feed the fire, he found A-Shi asleep awkwardly in the corner — his face toward the ground.

When he saw Lin Wen, A-Shi lowered his head and covered his face. "Uncle — I didn't keep it."

Lin Wen did not scold him. He pulled him up, steadied his shoulder, and motioned him to sit.

"How did the fire die?" he asked.

A-Shi: "I fell asleep."

Lin Wen: "The candle went out by itself — that was the candle. You fell asleep — that was you. Two things. Bound together — one failure."

A-Shi: "I didn't keep the fire."

Lin Wen: "It doesn't matter."

A-Shi: "How doesn't it matter?"

Lin Wen studied him for a while before answering. "Fire is no grand thing," he said. "It was never meant to be guarded like a treasure. Fire is a line that burns slowly."

He reached onto the table and drew out the last match that had never been struck — the one his mother had left for him to hold the fire through the night. He held it out to the boy.

A-Shi took it. He did not strike it at first.

"Fire is never kept by someone," Lin Wen said quietly. "Fire finds its own person. Failing to tend it once is not the worst thing in life. The man who loses a flame and still wants to kindle the next one — that man is the keeper."

A-Shi struck the match.

The flame rose.

He raised it level with his brow. A small, round flame — steady, soft, new. When the match burned down he set the spent stick beside the bowl.

"You see," Lin Wen said, "the last fire was kept by my mother's generation. This one is yours."

A-Shi bit his lip and stared at the bowl for a long time. He did it.

Morning arrived. Somewhere far off, a bird called, then folded its wings, then flew on.

Lin Wen poured a cup of cooled tea, set it into A-Shi's hands, and said quietly:

"My father, before he passed, spent every night of one year beside this hearth. The night even he could not hold out, he was still here the next morning to try again. That is what keeping the fire means — even when you lose it, you come back to it."

A-Shi held the cup carefully and asked:

"Does the keeper have to stay a whole night?"

Lin Wen: "No one has to. My father used to say — tending the fire is not for one person to stand a whole night. Tending is so that every morning, the house still has a light."

A-Shi left early.

Before he left, he lit the bowl's flame once, snuffed it, and lit it again — his small ceremony, his first oath sworn in air.

Lin Wen stood at the threshold watching his small departing figure disappear along the road, and understood what was happening in front of him: his generation would leave, but the fire in this house would not.

— the next generation's hand was already reaching for the match.
