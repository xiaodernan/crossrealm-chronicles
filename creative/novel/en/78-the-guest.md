## Seventy-eight: The Guest

Three weeks after the iron canister had found its groove in his father's workbench.

The stove burned steady as ever.

The fire-seed tree had stretched another inch upward; all seven needle-tips glowed dull red. Every morning small birds paused at the gate and moved on — no one knew what they were waiting for.

That afternoon, as A-Shi was laying new wood into the stove, someone came to the gate.

Not Baiyin.

A small girl, about seven, one shoe on and one off, carrying a tiny bundle over her shoulder, her forehead smudged with dried mud.

She stood at the threshold and stared at the stove without moving an inch.

"Are you the six hundred and first keeper?" she asked.

A-Shi blinked.

"Who told you that?"

The girl did not answer. She reached into her sleeve and pulled out a small piece of paper, and handed it to A-Shi — it held no words, only a rough, childishly drawn pair of eyes.

"Grandma Bai sent me," the girl said.

A-Shi's heart tightened.

Grandma Bai.

The words had not been spoken to him for years.

He took the paper. He did not know whose hand had drawn that pair of eyes — but he was certain he recognized something in them.

"What did your grandmother give you?" he asked.

The girl opened her little bundle and took out a small, yellowish parcel — careful, careful — inside it lay a single unburned coal, so dark it was almost black.

"Grandma Bai said your stove was short of fire."

A-Shi did not take it yet.

The girl continued:

"Our stove went out. My grandmother sent me over the ridge to borrow fire."

A-Shi was silent for a long moment.

Borrow fire — no one had ever said those words to him, not once, not in six hundred years of the keeper's silence.

He picked up a ladle of stove-ash water, dipped the little coal into it for a moment — the stove flared and the coal blazed to a tiny blue flame.

He wrapped the parcel up again and handed it back to the girl.

"Keep it shut," he said. "Don't open it."

The girl nodded. She stood quietly before the stove, arms held respectfully at her side.

"My grandmother said your flame is six hundred years old," the girl said.

A-Shi had no answer.

The girl tightened the strap of her parcel against her shoulder, turned, and walked away toward the mountain path. Halfway out the gate she turned back and said:

"My grandmother asked me to give you one sentence from her: the fire-seed walks on its own feet."

A-Shi stood at the stove for a long time after she was gone, with his palm resting on the stone ledge.

The warmth felt different than it had that morning — not a larger flare, but a deeper, steadier current of heat running the length of the iron canister, the groove, the workbench, and finally his own hand.

He lowered his head.

A seven-year-old girl had climbed a mountain to borrow fire for her family's stove — and as he stood there, something became terribly clear to him:

He did not hold this fire. The fire had been held — by all of them.

Six hundred years of keepers, and the fire had not only stayed. It had multiplied. It had spread from household to hearth to village to mountain ridge to the pine flats to a little girl's shoulder, and now it was going home again.

The stove was never only theirs.

It was never meant to be.

He fed a new log into the hearth and turned to his mother, who had returned from the drying rack and stood at the doorway watching.

"Mother," he said.

"Yes?"

"From now on," he said, "the stove-fire is not only ours."

His mother looked at him for a long moment.

Then she said, quietly:

"It never was."
