## Sixty-eight: The Gathering

Seven days after the kindle rite, his mother folded down where she stood by the stove.

A-Shi caught her arms before she could fall. Her palms were cold and clammy, her face drained of colour, yet her mind was perfectly clear. She said only:

"It's nothing. Just a rest and I'll be fine."

He carried her to her bed, then went back to the stove. He fed the fire a stick but it would not rouse itself. He stared at the hearth mouth for a long time before it struck him — from this day onward, every hour of every night would rest on his shoulders alone. The fire of six hundred years had landed in his hands at last.

Sleep would not come that night. Toward dawn he heard a soft knock at the door.

It was Uncle Ye at the threshold, a half-eaten roast chicken in his arms.

"The madam?" he asked.

"Resting."

"Good," said Uncle Ye. "While the hearth has a keeper, she can be at peace. Keep the fire low — I'll fetch her elder sister-in-law."

A-Shi wanted to say there was no need. Uncle Ye was already gone.

By mid-afternoon, Li Aunty arrived carrying a clay crock of congee. She stepped straight into the kitchen, said nothing, set the crock on the range to warm, and swept the stove-mouth with practiced eyes. She nodded once:

"The fire is well kept."

By dusk the old Daoist monk from the temple came, a flask of wine swinging from his hand. He sat on the threshold rather than go in, and said:

"Six hundred years the little flame has not gone out. The lamp at your crossroads — the temple will keep it burning yet."

Night came down, and by the time lanterns were lit, more had gathered around the stove than A-Shi expected. Uncle Ye, Li Aunty, the old Daoist, two or three neighbors from up the lane — all silently, and without invitation, standing close enough to the hearth to warm their hands. His mother had been helped in from her bed, bundled in her old cotton coat, propped up in a kitchen chair. The fire glowed orange, gilding every face and throwing their shadows tall against the wall.

No one spoke for a while. Then a log cracked like a whip and Uncle Ye said:

"Six hundred years ago the first fire was lit on winter solstice. Your great-grandfather was alive then."

"Yes," his mother said.

"That fire..."

"Was lit," she said, "and before the week was out, someone came over the ridge asking to borrow it. A traveling peddler, whose cart had sunk into the snow. He said he'd keep vigil on the ridge for the night. The old man gave him a coal without a second thought."

"And then?" asked Li Aunty.

His mother glanced at A-Shi.

"The peddler left the next day. As he went, he said: next time I pass through, I'll come back for this fire." She smiled. "He came back every year after that, each time asking to borrow the same coal. The ever-burning lamp at the crossroads — he lit it with it. The beacon on the ridge — he persuaded the old man to build it with it."

"So your family's fire..." Uncle Ye began.

"Has always been a fire," his mother said, "lent to others. A keeper does not keep fire from going out — a keeper keeps fire so that others may carry some of it away."

The stove grew brighter.

A light step in the doorway — everyone turned. Baiyin stood in the entrance, in a moon-white robe washed pale with time. Her hair was a little longer than the last time she had stood here, and her eyes in the firelight were as still as deep wells.

"Baiyin," said his mother.

Baiyin nodded, walked to the stove, glanced in, and said:

"The fire is steady."

She lowered herself beside A-Shi, much as she had once sat waiting on the white stone.

No one else spoke. The logs cracked and sighed. Uncle Ye set a pair of chopsticks in front of A-Shi. Li Aunty spooned congee into five bowls. Baiyin took two of them — one for his mother, one for A-Shi. The broth was hot and barely salted, nothing but rice and water. Yet no one said anything as they ate, only stared into the fire.

When the night was deep and the guests began to drift home, the old Daoist left the flask of wine on the stove ledge, and Uncle Ye left the bones of the chicken with it. As Baiyin rose to leave, she said to his mother:

"Six hundred years your fire has not faltered. This generation won't either."

His mother nodded.

At the door Baiyin paused, turned back, and said to A-Shi softly:

"Six hundred years. Every time someone stands before this fire, the fire looks back."

A-Shi stood by the stove for a long while, watching.

It opened in him, slowly.

Gathering around the stove is not a family warming itself — it is six hundred years of people meeting, year after year, before the same hearth — some come, some go, the fire burns on, the people turn over like the seasons.

He put one final stick on the embers and said to his mother:

"I understand now."

"What is it you understand?" she asked.

"This fire," A-Shi said, "is not something I keep. It's something that keeps me."

His mother said nothing. She drew her coat tighter around her shoulders. Night deepened. The stove burned on. There would come another day, soon, when another would come bringing wood, and sit around this same flame.

A-Shi kept watch before it until dawn.

The fire did not go out.
