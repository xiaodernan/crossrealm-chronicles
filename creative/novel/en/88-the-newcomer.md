## Eighty-eight: The Newcomer

On the second morning after the snow, the door opened once.

A boy was standing in the doorway.

Eight or nine years old, head shaved, robes washed to the colour of ash. He held one hand pressed into his own sleeve, the other wrapped around nothing — a child who had not learned yet to bring anything except himself.

His eyes went first to A-Shi, then to the fire, then back to A-Shi.

"Is this your fire?" he asked.

A-Shi nodded.

The boy reached into his sleeve and brought out a folded slip of paper — no bigger than two fingers wide, folded so tightly it was almost worn through.

He unfolded it with both hands.

Written on it in brush strokes so young and unsteady they were almost unreadable:

I am small fire.

A-Shi turned the paper over. On the back, in a different hand — neater, older:

Number Six Hundred and Two.

A-Shi did not move.

The boy did not move either. He waited the way only someone raised in this valley could wait — trained to silence, trained to stay exactly where he was, trained to never go past the threshold until the fire gave him leave.

"Where did you come from?" A-Shi asked.

The boy pointed without speaking, toward the ridge.

"I stood outside all night," he said. "I was afraid to come in."

A-Shi felt something open in him.

The boy's voice was not quavering. He was steady as the stove, practiced as a monk, still as a lantern in a closed room. There was nothing uncertain in him at all.

"I am small fire," the boy said quietly, "because Grandma Bai sent me."

A-Shi did not answer.

The stove leapt.

The room flooded so white it might have been noon in August — and inside that light, in the folds of shadow at the edge of the kitchen, A-Shi saw himself as clearly as he had ever seen anything. His mother grown old beyond recognition. His grandmother who never aged. The boy grown into a man he had not yet met. Every keeper's face stacked inside this stove — she stood behind him and answered for all of them.

He understood everything then.

The boy stepped inside the house.

He went to the stove, knelt, and opened his small wrapped bundle — inside lay a single dark ember, cold as a pebble.

He held it out toward A-Shi.

"Grandmother Bai told me to bring it," the boy said. "You already have yours."

A-Shi reached for the iron canister on the stove's ledge.

The coal inside had gone not dark, but bright.

He looked at the boy.

"Your grandmother sent you a name," A-Shi said.

The boy nodded.

"She sent you two."

A-Shi took out the coal. He put it beside the one the boy had brought.

The two coals lay together on the stove's iron lip — two grains of fire separated by six hundred years, meeting for the first time.

The boy said, very quietly:

"They are the same grandmother."

A-Shi did not ask what he meant.

He placed both coals inside the hearth.

The stove did not merely flare.

It bloomed — a full orange-white exhalation, every grain of pitch and every splinter of pine in the stove catching at once, the whole fire-family rising together, all of them seeing all of them, six hundred years stored in one perfect instant of light —

And then settling, together, back into one warm, breathing flame.

Outside, across the ridge and across the valley, every fire that still burned responded in kind — the ever-burning lamp, the beacon on the white stone, every stove and every pipe and every candle across every mountain line —

Six hundred years, one breath, everyone holding hands in the same light.

The boy sat down beside A-Shi at the stove's edge, the way every keeper before him had done.

He smiled at the fire.

He did not ask A-Shi to teach him.

The fire was already teaching him.
