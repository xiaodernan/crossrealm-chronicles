## Thirty-six: The Fourth Pavilion

The fourth pavilion had fallen years before Lin Wen reached it.

Time and weather had finished what men could no longer mend — a shear from some forgotten flood, a year of rain, the slow collapse of old brick and pine. When he arrived, the light was almost gone from the sky. He looked around him and smiled: the ink-map had painted all four pavilions clearly enough. The first was the low oil-lamp pavilion, the second the pavilion that fed the apple, the third the pavilion Sister Gu kept, and this one — the last mark on the map — where the line of red cinnabar stopped.

The oil lamp he had been promised was gone.

In his palm, the iron token began to warm.

He pushed aside the fallen stones and came into the ruin. Inside the walls there was only one remnant — a single stone face, and in its center a shallow recess, no deeper than a hand. Inside the recess lay one object:

A pair of fire-tongs, edge gone, only yellow-brown metal left, with a few strands of grey hair still curled around their tip.

Lin Wen set the iron token inside the recess.

It answered with a sound no louder than a struck pebble — not metal, not bell: fire. A blue ghost of steam rose from inside it, and that steam turned to light, and the light rose higher, wider, and then faded.

And in that one held breath of light, he saw the ash on the wall bloom into a single character —

**火。**

Written with soot mixed with grey — a practised, familiar hand, like the strokes that had filled the ledger for eighteen years. The character formed fast and slow at once, as if greeting him, as if finishing a promise that had been made before he was born.

In the stone recess there was a rolled black sheet.

He unrolled it.

His father had left only two characters on it:

**林渡。**

Not his father's hand. 墨鹮's.

Lin Wen wept then. He hung his head, rain gathering in the crevices of the broken stones. Wind came up from the south, and the small flame in the recess guttered once and then was steadied again by someone nearby, or someone looking on from very far away.

He did not fall. But his hands were shaking.

He wiped his face and stood up. He took the broken tongs in his hand — those tongs had cropped the wicks of lamps across more generations of keepers than these mountains had winters. Their broken edge was the witness of a promise kept badly, or maybe only once, at a cost.

He drew from his pocket the strip of bark he had carried from the old persimmon tree, and laid it deliberately across the broken tongs.

He said, very quietly:

"Father. I'll finish it."

Wind came suddenly through the seam in the wall. The fire in the recess turned in a small, perfect circle — as if someone, far above him, had closed their hand around the flame and accepted what he was offering.

The fourth pavilion was not destroyed at all. The flame in the recess — small, almost invisible — stayed lit through the night. It did not die.

At dawn, when Lin Wen walked out of the ruin, his shadow stretched far behind him. It projected a road across the wet grass that ran a straight line north — toward the wall that opened once a generation for exactly one person.

A bell he could not see rang once, very far away. Not far enough to be outside hearing.

At the end of the road was the gate.
