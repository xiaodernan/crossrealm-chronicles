## Fifty: The Tenth Year

In the winter of his tenth year, the candle in the clay bowl went out.

He had fallen asleep without meaning to. When he woke, the room was dark and the wax had slumped into a translucent stub and gone cold.

He was terrified.

The fire had gone out on the night he was supposed to be keeping it.

His mother had gone to visit Du the Elder — his whole family was scattered across the valley, too far to return in time. Lin Wen was away. No one else was in the house.

He sat in front of the bowl for a long time. At first he thought the fire was simply sleeping. He put his ear to the dish and listened. Nothing moved.

He wanted to cry — he did not. Instead, he went to the cupboard and found a small carton of matches. Only five were left.

He struck one — nothing. Another — nothing. Third — nothing.

His hand was shaking before the fourth. Then he remembered what Lin Wen had told him:

"Fire is not a god. Fire demands that you bring it yourself."

He steadied his breathing. He folded his fingers the way his father had shown him, and struck the match firmly.

The flame leapt out.

He carried it to the candle dish. The wax melted; the wick caught.

That night he did not cry.

He sat before the bowl, watching the flame flicker and lean — his ten-year-old eyes — and he did not blink until the first rooster crowed beyond the ridge.

In the morning he counted the five matches again.

Four were gone. One remained.

He held the last match for a long time. Then he tucked it into the seam of his pillow.

The next morning, his mother came with a cloth to wipe the bowl. She stopped, looked at A-Shi for a long moment — almost nothing, and yet she saw everything.

She said nothing. He said nothing either.

But from that day forward, the matchbox was never empty when the bowl was tended.

A-Shi understood one thing completely that day: when fire dies — you strike another one.

He never forgot that night. Years later, on a moonless night of wind, he lent out the last match from the seam of his pillow — not to light his own bowl — to light someone else's.

When he struck that match, he saw his grandfather's silhouette, standing by the doorway, holding court by the fire.

He did not cry then, either — not the same way.

He did not need to anymore.

Fire had not died. Fire had only been waiting for him to choose to strike again.

That person — the one who strikes the match even when no one is watching — is the keeper of the fire.

He is also the fourth in the family line.
