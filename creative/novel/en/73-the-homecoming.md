## Seventy-three: The Homecoming

Ten days after the reply was written, A-Shi rose every morning earlier than the household.

He did not say who he was waiting for. He only built the fire, sat by the stove, and watched the road beyond the gate as the snow slowly melted.

On the seventh morning, his mother was airing the quilts in the courtyard when she saw a thin blue light feather out from the stove-room. She turned and looked in.

An old man was sitting on the threshold.

Very old — so old his hair looked like two logs of dead winter wood.

In his hand he turned over and over a small oil lamp — its wick burned down to the very end, and the oil reservoir bone dry. The whole lamp had gone black.

He did not greet A-Shi first. He looked at the stove for a long time, and only then said:

"It is still burning."

"It is still burning," A-Shi answered.

The old man nodded. He lowered his eyes to the lamp once more, his voice very quiet:

"This lamp... I have borrowed for six hundred years."

A-Shi understood everything in that one breath.

This lamp was the very one lit from the first spark borrowed from this stove six hundred years ago — the lamp lit at the crossroads, the ever-burning one, still burning to this day.

Not still burning. Running dry.

The borrower had simply never dared to return it.

The old man set the lamp down on the hearthside, straightening it as carefully as a man setting out an offering for an ancestor.

"Six hundred years ago I promised your great-great-grandfather — when I have lit the ever-burning lamp at the crossroads, I will come back and return this lamp. I set out that day. I crossed the mountain, and then I crossed another, and I walked for one year, and then another, and year after year for six hundred years. Every winter, every single winter, I saw this stove burning from far away. I never once forgot it."

He stopped.

"I did not fail to return because I could not. I could not return because I could not dare to enter this house."

"Six hundred years. The stove has never once gone out. As long as the stove burns, my debt stays unsettled."

A-Shi listened to his voice, and it was like the wind blowing through six hundred years of snow — ancient, crystalline, and carrying a tremor so slight it was nearly nothing at all.

The old man said:

"The last time I stood on this ridge, I looked once more at the stove burning far below. That was when I finally understood: fire does not burn for the reason that someone owes it a debt — it burns because that is what it is made for, and that is what it does. The ones who keep it, and the fire itself, they are already together. We have always been together for six hundred years."

He took off his hat and revealed a face so old the eyes had worn nearly level with the skin.

"Six hundred years ago I left this house with an empty basket on my back. I have come back now..."

From his sleeve, he drew a small parcel and placed it beside the lamp.

"This is for your family."

A-Shi opened the parcel. Inside were a few dried pine needles — yellow, dry, and brittle — and yet their scent had not gone: pine smoke and campfire, mingled gently together.

"These are the fire-seeds," said the old man. "Six hundred years ago, your great-great-grandfather gave me the first coal. I picked up a few pine needles on my way out, hoping to start a fire with them when I got home. They walked with me through six hundred years. I kept them all this time."

He smiled faintly:

"Six hundred years, and they still have not gone grey."

A-Shi set the needles by the stove and did not speak.

His mother came in then, stood behind the old man, and looked at the stove.

The flame leapt.

Softly — a tongue of fire curled up and then sank back.

Nothing caught light; nothing was burned. But A-Shi felt something inside the room settle into place, like a six-hundred-year-old wooden chair finally being pushed back into the corner where it had always belonged.

The old man sat on the threshold and drank a bowl of plain rice porridge his mother had brought him.

He did not speak for a long while.

When dusk came down, he stood and said to his mother:

"Thank you for sheltering me tonight."

"Tomorrow I must go,"

He stopped halfway through his sentence. He looked at the stove again, as if he could see six hundred years of this family standing there — Grandfather, Father, Mother, A-Shi, and the forgotten faces of the ones before them.

"Tonight is enough," he said.

He stepped out into the snow. From the gateway, A-Shi watched him follow the mountain path — walking far, further than any man his age should have been able to walk — and he never once looked back.

The stove-fire grew a little brighter.

His mother collected the old man's bowl and chopsticks. She gathered the pine needles and placed them in the wooden box, which she had not opened for six hundred years.

"Six hundred years," she said. "And these still did not go grey."

A-Shi looked at the stove.

"They are not needles," he said. "They are a debt, uncollected. A six-hundred-year-old debt, and fire."

The flame answered with one small bright waver.
