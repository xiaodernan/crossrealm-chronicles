## One Hundred and Nine: The Delivery of Ashes

On the morning before the spring equinox, the ice in the courtyard was gone.

Icicles fell from the eaves overnight; the water film on the stone steps was already dry by sunrise. The needles of the fire-seed tree were just sprouting — a deeper green this year, and a brighter one.

A-Shi's mother called Xiao Huo to her side.

"Choose a small porcelain jar," she said. "Fill it with ash from our stove."

Xiao Huo chose a jar. He packed it with stove-ash, pressed it down with great care. Only when he was done did he look at his mother and wait for her to say one thing: fill it instead with the ancestor-ash.

She did not say that.

She fetched from the iron box the slip of paper that had said "the fifteenth — see you next year" — the one dated for the mid-autumn a year ago. It had been lying in the box since then, unanswered. She studied it for a long while.

"Ours will do," she said.

Xiao Huo understood. He did not press the question further.

He wrapped the jar in a strip of grey cloth — the same cloth that had tied the nameless twig. Three beige patches. It looked, Xiao Huo thought, exactly the kind of cloth meant for a delivery.

A-Shi washed a square of white cloth and tied the bundle with a clean knot.

His mother added a small packet of wild scallions.

"You can't go up the mountain and eat nothing," she said.

They waited.

Three months had passed since the mid-autumn mentioned on that slip of paper. The "fifteenth" it had promised had come and gone, and no bundle had appeared; nothing else had been said. But on this particular spring equinox morning, the gate to the courtyard rattled once on its own.

When they went out to the gate, no one was there.

Only one bundle — smaller than ever before, each twig thinner — sitting on the driest stone in the courtyard.

A-Shi's mother knelt and picked it up. She turned it over once.

Inside, a slip of paper so thin it was translucent as a cicada wing.

"For the little firekeeper."

No name.

"It doesn't feel like someone else," Xiao Huo whispered. "It feels like our own stove talking about us — from outside."

No one answered. Some things the stove does — or the world does around it — the house never names.

A-Shi's mother folded the slip and placed it in the basket along with the jar.

A-Shi carried the basket up the mountain.

The path was the same path he had climbed twice already. He remembered every stone. The small stone house on the white stone ridge stood as it had — no taller, no wider — but the old man himself seemed lower, like a tree set in old snow; last spring his shadow had reached the eaves; this spring it stopped at the doorway.

He set the basket on the step.

The old man untied it and looked inside for a long time. He did not ask whose ash was inside.

Neither did his mother ask.

He lifted the jar with both hands and felt the rim with his fingertips. A thin film of frost had settled on the lid. His fingertips paused for a moment and then moved away.

He looked at A-Shi and said:

"At the next equinox, we will pass the day together."

A-Shi wanted to nod but did not quite nod.

Instead he asked the question he had never dared to ask before:

"Does the ancestor-fire miss home?"

The old man did not answer.

He reached into the basket and felt the packet of wild scallions — then smiled.

"It has seen the stove," he said. "It has been watching this house."

He did not open the black packet of ash. He carried the jar inside the stone house.

A-Shi descended the mountain in the last light of the day.

The fire-seed tree moved gently in the wind. Its new needles shimmered with the first tinge of summer.

When A-Shi stepped back into the gate with the empty basket on his back, the stove leapt once inside.

The fire had been burning higher than it had the week before.

Now it jumped again — not a lurch, just a quiet, tall greeting. The stove knew.

Seven hundred years.

The first spring.

They were all sending each other their ashes.
