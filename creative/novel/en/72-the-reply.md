# The Unchanted Chronicle of Crossrealm: The Keeper of the Anchor Fire

## Seventy-two: The Reply

Five days after the painter left, A-Shi opened the wooden box and took out the kindle-jar to polish it.

The wax seal on the lid — sealed six hundred years ago — was perfectly intact. He sat by the stove with the jar in his lap, and then he noticed something: a tiny folded page lay at the bottom of the jar, where there had never been anything before.

The paper was torn from the ledger — its edges charred black.

He unfolded it.

One single line, written in pine-soot ink, small but pressed hard enough to mark the back of the paper:

The debt is not fire. Your grandfather's grandfather's grandfather owes me a reply.

A-Shi froze.

He read the line three times. The sky outside was clear, the stove-fire steady as a well. He almost called out to his mother — but then he stopped himself, because he understood this letter was not addressed to her. It was for him.

"Six hundred years," he said aloud, "and he is still waiting."

He folded the page and slipped it back into the jar, then took the painter's seven-stroke painting down from its place and spread both on the kitchen table. The seven strokes pointed outward in every direction — like a flame, like ridge lines, like open hands.

He thought hard.

Six hundred years ago, a peddler crossed the mountain and came to this very stove. He borrowed a coal to light the crossroads' ever-burning lamp. He came back every year after, to borrow the same stove-fire — and every year he came, for six hundred years.

Why could a man walk for six hundred years and never arrive?

Because someone who can walk for six hundred years is not a peddler at all. He is someone who is waiting for a reply.

"What is he waiting for?" A-Shi asked his mother.

She was trimming vegetables, not looking up.

"When someone owes you something you never settle," she said, "you will wait forever for the settlement."

"What did he owe?"

She set down the knife and stood still for a long time before answering.

"Six hundred years ago, your great-great-grandfather gave the peddler a coal. The peddler accepted it, and he said something before he left."

"What did he say?"

She was silent for a while.

"He said: when I have lit the ever-burning lamp at the crossroads, I will come back and return this coal."

"And then?"

"He did light it," said his mother. "The first ever-burning lamp at the crossroads — it was lit with that same coal. And from that day forward, he never came back. He climbed the mountain instead. He never returned. Six hundred years — every house burns the lamp in his honour, since nobody dared to forget him. But he himself has never crossed this threshold again."

"What is he waiting for?"

She looked at A-Shi for a long moment.

"For the fire to go out," she said. "He thought the fire would eventually go out. When the fire went out, the debt would be settled."

A-Shi stood very still.

"But the fire never went out."

"And that is why he cannot return," she said. "Six hundred years he has not come home — not because the fire went out, but because it never did. He never dared to face it."

A-Shi stood for a long time afterward without saying anything.

Then he sat down — took the painter's painting and the charred fragment of ledger page, set them side by side on the table, and drew from his pocket a small jar of pine-soot ink and a clean brush — a brush he had never used before, hand-whittled from a piece of stove-wood. He touched the brush into the heart of the stove, where the coals glowed hottest, and let the ash mingle with the ink. Then he wrote on the margin of the seven-stroke painting, in his own hand, nobody else's:

The fire received the message. The debt is not fire — it is six hundred years of people who kept this door for you. The stove-fire will not go out, the ever-burning lamp will not dim, and the white stone on the ridge is still lit. Six hundred years have passed. It is time to come home. The stove is still here.

He folded the painting and slid it into the kindle-jar beside the ledger fragment, and sealed the jar back in the wooden box.

As dusk came down, the stove leapt.

The flames shot up so tall they nearly reached the beam above. A-Shi looked up into the hearth, and for an instant he saw the whole stove-fire flare like a great warm wind — and something light and bright passed through it, gone before the eye could catch it. A shadow shaped like an old man, moving light and easy — but this time, not up the mountain. Back down.

No one spoke.

The fire steadied again slowly. A-Shi felt the whole kitchen grow a touch brighter.

Six hundred years, and the debt was a single letter.

Six hundred years, and the reply was this.
