# Interlude Seven · Come Sit Awhile

**Written after *Interlude Six · Handover*: this volume is neither prologue nor epilogue.**
**It is the origin of the paper on the wall.**

---

## I · Nobody Knows Who Pinned the Paper

The paper on the wall of the waystation carries no name, no date, and no seal.

Flip through every old ledger — the earliest page reads *“The lamping still happens.”* Page by page the hand changes, ink by ink the line changes, and yet the sentence never does. It says only this:

**“We take no letters here — only travellers, come sit a while.”**

The new one once asked who had written it. The old keeper said that whoever did was long gone. And then he added: the sentence is still here.

The bottom-left corner of the paper has been worn to a soft radius — not by the shape of paper, but by the shape of hands.

---

## II · Under the Paper

The paper says *no letters.* The space under the paper has never been clean.

Someone tucked in a sock folded small. Someone tucked in a crumpled ferry ticket. Someone tucked in a drawing of boots — boots worn through, laces long since given up. Someone tucked in half a math problem, the pen strokes pressed hard, the answer unwritten.

The old keeper never read them.

And he never cleared them out.

The wind scattered them, the rain softened them, the sun dried them back to crisp. The travellers who tuck the scraps into the wall-gap know exactly what they are tucking in. The waystation does not collect drawings; it collects paper. The flame does not read them, and the ash will not carry them away.

The paper knows where it goes. The paper always knows.

---

## III · The One Who Left “Next Time” Behind

One year, late in the cold season, a woman arrived with dust on her boots.

The dust was so old the road below her had long since stopped asking. She sat, she finished half a warm meal, chewed the last bite slowly, stood, and tucked a scrap of paper into the wall-gap —

**Next time.**

Not *next time I'll come.* Not *thank you.* Just *next time.*

The old keeper looked at the scrap for a long while. Then he began wiping the furnace door.

That night the furnace burned a little warmer than usual.

---

## IV · Why Hearthmen Never Write Their Names

The waystation ledger has never held a name. Only the time, the lamp, and the page.

Time blurs the boundaries between one shift and the next. Someone remembers the lamp they lit in the small hours; someone remembers who came before them — whoever remembers first is the one who loses, and the one who still has to stay.

When the old keeper's shift came to its end, the new one picked up the pencil cup. He did not write his own name into it. He set the next shift's pencil back.

Names are for apprentices. The waystation does not need apprentices.

---

## V · The Letter That Mattered

Another year, someone came carrying a letter.

The letter was written for someone who was already gone — to a mother. The letter-writer knew she would one day walk on; the letter was to hang in the waystation, waiting for the one who would come *next time.*

The old keeper followed the letter of the rule. He did not take the letter. He would not deliver it. The paper on the wall said: no letters accepted.

But the old keeper folded the letter and tucked it into the wall-gap — not to leave the letter in the waystation, but to leave a road-marker there.

Years later, a traveller who had never once come that way passed through. In the wall-gap she felt a folded letter. She did not look at the sender; she did not look at the address. She simply opened it, read the handwriting she did not recognize, folded it back exactly as she found it — and looked at it one more time.

The paper knows where it goes. The traveller knows who it was for.

---

## VI · For Everyone Who Cannot Sit in the Chair

If you're reading this —

**You don't have to have been there. You only have to have never been there.**

Some people never made it to the waystation. Some arrived and only dared to look at the wall, never dared to sit. Some came only to see one line of words — to believe, or not to believe, as long as it tells them the waystation is still there.

The wall was for them.

The waystation takes no letters — this is said for *them*: **you don't have to come. You've already come. The scraps are yours; the chair was yours; the night was yours.**

Come sit a while.
