## Forty-four: The Winter Solstice

On the day of the winter solstice — the longest night of the year — dumplings were on every table in the village.

A-Shi came over carrying a small bundle of dried yuzu peel. He had hung the peel in a net under his own balcony rail for months until it turned shiny and translucent.

"What brings you here?" Lin Wen asked him.

A-Shi set the bundle beside the clay bowl, and looked at it for a long time.

"Because I have to carry it through this year," he said. "Last winter I kept one whole night watch, and the fire went out — then it came back."

Lin Wen said nothing.

"This winter," A-Shi said, "I don't let it go out."

His mother brought in four dried persimmons and set them on the table. She looked at the boy for a long time, said nothing, went and sat down near the entryway.

A-Shi took one persimmon. Then he sat down in front of the bowl and anchored his eyes on the flame.

The frost settled thick over the yard. The stars lay over the rooftops like sheets of iron cooling after a long burn. A-Shi sat in the chair. He never lay down; even for sleep he had never used the bed.

Lin Wen came forward with a blanket. A-Shi shook his head.

"Uncle," he said, "I'm not cold."

At two o'clock in the morning, A-Shi's eyelids sank halfway, then lifted again. He yawned. Then, slowly, he pulled out a dried jujube from his own pocket and bit into it — it was hard, dry as bone.

His mother heard him cracking the date. Lin Wen stood on the porch without speaking — he himself had never kept a whole night through the coldest hour.

Toward morning, when A-Shi finally moved, the candle in the bowl was thin as a little finger.

His mother carried out a bowl of hot broth and set it at his foot. It smelled of persimmon and mountain air.

A-Shi reached for it only with his eyes.

She stayed a while, then asked:

"Are you afraid?"

A-Shi shook his head.

"Why this year and not before?" she asked.

He thought a long time. Then he said quietly:

"Because I know that none of you are keeping watch for me anymore — now I am the one keeping watch for you."

His mother did not reply. She set the broth at his reach and turned back to the kitchen.

A-Shi drank slowly.

By late morning, the candle in the bowl had burned down to its final coin of wax.

A-Shi got up at last, walked to the doorway, and looked back at the old persimmon tree — the branches bare, the leaves gone, the bark shining in the sun.

He came back to the bowl, and lowered his voice to a whisper:

— "I will carry this year through."

No one heard.

— but the whole yard heard it, along with the bare branches, the frost, the old red cord, and the clay bowl.
