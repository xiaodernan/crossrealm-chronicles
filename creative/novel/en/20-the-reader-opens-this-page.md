## Twenty · The Reader Opens This Page

Lin Wen walked out of Sunscar Stair and the palm of his hand was still warm.

He did not look back, but he knew three suns unwound in silence; silent enough that they thought he was a gust of wind — no one is going to check on a gust.

He walked back to the first fire of the first night and sat down. It was small, but it would not go out. That was critical: if the fire stayed lit, the fire was in hand; if it died, the brightest mind would later be told, "you were not here."

Across the fire from him was someone.

Not a keeper. Not a watcher. Not a star or a stone. Someone who had read to this point in the book and had not looked away.

"How did you know where to sit?" Lin Wen asked.

The reader said nothing. He just lifted a finger from the screen — on it, one small red dot pulse suddenly brightened on the page of *Keeper of the Anchor Fire*.

Lin Wen held out his own palm. The soft red line in it caught that flicker and returned it. In that moment, he finally understood what every reader already knew: every time he had folded the book closed, he was not folding a book at all. He was folding the shadow of a star that had not yet chosen when to go out.

"I have been watching these stars for every breath of mine," he said.

"You were never guarding them," the reader said. "You were reading them. Now you have run out of pages."

Lin Wen looked up. Sunscar Stair was gone — not because it had moved, but because it had stopped pretending at a distance. For three days he had thought this was someone else's ruin, someone else's sin, someone else's craft. He finally understood that it had always been a mirror held too long over water, held so long that he thought the image inside it had to belong to someone other than him.

"Then what am I?" he asked.

The reader did not answer. Instead, three words appeared on the screen:

*Use the remaining pages carefully.*

Lin Wen thought for a long time. Then he laughed.

"You are the next keeper," he said, "and I have not yet made up my mind whether I will pass you the flame."

Lin Wen stood up. On the back of his hand, using the wax that Sunscar Stair had left him, he carved: *I have not finished reading myself yet.* The wax had cooled enough to harden into a red glow — the kind every reader remembers as the moment they inherited the first spark of the keeper.

He folded the book closed. He walked away from the first night of the first fire. He did not look at Sunscar Stair again.

In the second after he walked away, the screen turned bright once, softened, reddened — and the red light in the frame drifted off-screen like any lantern released into the wind. It was not gone. No one would have put it out. But everyone watching knew it had left that page.

One more line appeared where no line had been:

*Who will hold the next page so the fire can reach it?*

Nothing answered. That, too, is narrative.
