## Thirty-four: The Waystation

When spring reached the waystation, Lin Wen set out south with his father's ledger.

He left the house carrying one more thing — a thin curl of bark scraped from the old persimmon tree, which he tucked into the clay altar bowl, between the persimmon leaf and the folded blue scarf. His mother had only agreed to let the scarf return to the slope on the Start of Spring; he didn't dare move it himself, but the bark she did not object to.

On the third day, he came to the mountain valley from where his father had always mailed his letters: 遥火驿, Yáohuǒ Yì — the Ember Post, the mountain station at the mouth of the old northern road.

It sat inside a narrow throat between two ridgelines, the stones worn mirror-flat by centuries of sandals and hooves. The doorframe sagged to the left; a single old copper bell hung above it, and when the wind stirred, the bell spoke with a low, waterlogged note.

Outside, an old man smoked a pipe. Hill people rarely speak more than they need to. Lin Wen called to him. The old man did not look up. Only when Lin Wen laid his father's ledger open on his knee, at the last page, did the man draw in one long breath and open his eyes.

"So you're the son," he said.

Lin Wen nodded.

The old man lifted his chin toward the loft at the far end of the station.

"He came here every year for ten years, one letter a year. The last one said — give it two or three years, and his son will come looking."

Lin Wen climbed the loft ladder.

The room was small. One stone wall. A long-handled oil lamp hanging from a beam in the southern way — the kind that swings with wind — and beneath it, a box of black lacquer. A red cord wound it round and round, tight enough that the wood had hollowed at the knot. Resting on the cord was a folded slip of paper — his father's hand:

**Three letters, inked in black on black. The scribe was 墨鹮, not I. Open them after spring.**

Lin Wen untied the cord.

Inside were three letters — paper soaked in black water, the ink indistinguishable at first under plain light, readable only in candlefire. He angled the lamp closer; the strokes lifted faintly out of the dark, one by one.

The first letter began with a single word across its head:

**墨鹮**

And beneath, one small line:

*I have split the fire into three branches. One at the North Pass. One to the south, at the waystation of 遥火. One is in your hands, now. If my son walks here, give him all three.*

Lin Wen looked at the next line of characters. Beneath the ink sat a thin sheet of silk with an arc of cinnabar route painted on it — one end marked 遥火驿. The other end carried a single character:

**火**

The second letter was only the map.

Drawn in mineral ink, the route rose from the waystation toward the northeast, along a broken ridge-line where a ruined pavilion stood every ninety-one li. The line ended against the silhouette of a high wall. Beneath it, in letters that seemed to have been carved into the paper with the end of a flint, were these characters:

**This gate opens once every generation.**

Lin Wen stared for a long time.

He still didn't understand what his father had wanted him to come here for. Only the third letter answered it.

The third letter was unsealed.

Only a single handprint sat where the seal should have been — a palm pressed with lampblack, light as a crumpled page. Beneath it, a few lines in his father's hand:

*I walked the North Pass once. I did not reach the gate. The next keeper will come of age; you are coming back. 墨鹮 kept the third branch of the fire for you. I did not write it. The sky did.*

Lin Wen folded the three letters in order. He did not laugh. He did not cry. He only watched the oil lamp sway when the draft came from the north.

The next morning, when he told his mother he had to go, she did not try to stop him.

She stood in the doorway a long time and said:

"Your father is the kind of man who protects what he can."

Lin Wen bowed once. He shouldered the box, the altar bark, and his own pen. He stepped out onto the blue-stone road that ran north.

The road from 遥火驿 was quiet, the stones of it worn to a shine, the air rich with straw, old paper, and the ghost of a fire that had been sleeping for a long time.

He walked north, the box on his back heavy in exactly the way it was meant to be.
