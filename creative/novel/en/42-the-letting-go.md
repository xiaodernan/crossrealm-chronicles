## Forty-two: The Letting Go

In the tail of autumn, Lin Wen's mother set off to visit her sister for seven days. Before leaving she told Lin Wen one thing:

"The bowl — you watch it."

A light rain fell over the courtyard that night. The old persimmon tree had scattered its leaves across the stones, each one breathing like a distant hill. Lin Wen stayed on the porch a long time, until half past three he stood up and began to listen.

There was no lamp on the porch. All he could hear was rain in small threads. In the doorway dimness, a small shape sat beside the clay bowl.

It was A-Shi.

A-Shi had not noticed him. He sat very straight, his back straighter than Lin Wen's own. The candle had burned to a stump. In A-Shi's right hand he held a single unstruck match, staring at the flame with fierce concentration.

He brought the match close to the flame. He stopped. He did not strike it.

The rain softened to nothing. A single leaf fell from the tree.

— Lin Wen understood then: A-Shi did not want to light the fire.

— He wanted to know whether he could bear the moment.

Lin Wen drew one deep breath and called his name.

A-Shi turned. His eyes went wide. He crushed the match into his palm and stood up very suddenly.

"You didn't sleep?" he asked.

"I was keeping the fire too," Lin Wen said.

"Then I won't strike it."

"A-Shi — strike it," Lin Wen said. "Whether I'm watching or not doesn't matter. What you do here belongs to it."

A-Shi laughed softly. Then he struck the match.

The flame leapt up. Its light grazed his boyish face — he lifted the match up and out, and held himself steady.

Then he planted the cooled shaft into the clay soil by the door frame, where it stood straight as a spear.

— this was his first living match.

Lin Wen did not ask. He thought of something his mother had once said to him at this hearth — something his father had left behind for him to finish without anyone noticing.

Lin Wen paused for a long breath before he answered. He had been carrying this sentence for years. He set it down at last:

"The fire is theirs to live, A-Shi. You don't keep it. You let them take turns."

A-Shi did not reply.

He stood for a very long time at the threshold, in front of the hearth.

His mother came home on the fourth day. She pushed open the door. Lin Wen met her in the kitchen.

"Mother — A-Shi left something."

A-Shi had come by earlier with a small bundle of dried persimmon leaves, tied with a shoot from the old tree, pressed flat. On top of the bundle sat a little paper box, labeled in his stiff, childish hand:

**The Fire Does Not Go Out.**

With a strip of cloth knotted on top.

"For when you need to light it again," the box said.

He had made a persimmon-leaf torch.

Lin Wen accepted it without speaking. He looked at his mother. She said only — she had known everything all along, just as mothers do.

Months passed. The old persimmon tree let one more leaf fall.

And that was the day Lin Wen set the bundle beside the clay bowl, and let it rest there.

— the fire did not go out.

— did the flame shrink?

— No. It simply took a different match.
