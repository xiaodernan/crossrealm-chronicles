## Fifty-eight: The White Stone

Early spring, the river was thawing.

A-Shi rode down to the bank for the first time. He knew the road had led him here. He carried an empty clay bowl — nothing inside; he had not told anyone about the bowl.

He stopped at the stones.

There was a girl on the far bank.

White clothes, white hair like seed down — a face he had never seen and would somehow never forget.

She saw him watching, and she called across:

"You are the child Lin took back with the bowl?"

A-Shi stared.

Yes. He was.

"They call you A-Shi," she said.

"I have that name."

She smiled. Quiet white smile.

"I am Baiyin," she said.

"Of where?"

"I guard the river upstream," she said. "My house is beside this river."

A-Shi did not ask how long she had been there.

She drew her hand out of her sleeve and offered him one single white stone — simple as a pebble, smaller than a coat button.

"Take it," she said.

A-Shi took it.

"What is this?"

"It is from your grandfather," she said.

A-Shi almost dropped it.

He looked at the stone carefully, turned it in his palm.

"Then it belonged to him?"

"Then it belongs to you," she said.

She took a step and began to leave.

"How do you know my grandfather?" A-Shi called after her.

She did not stop.

He looked down. The stone was already warm.

Baiyin was gone. The river surface carried nothing of her presence.

He stood very still, his grandfather's silent ah in his palm.

Then he took the bowl out of the pouch, removed the stone from his pocket, and put it inside.

The bowl began to glow from within.

A faint, strange warmth traveled outward — upward — the way fire does.

By the time he realized it, his fingers were warm and his heart had quieted.

When he reached his own home, A-Shi put the bowl on the table as though it had never been moved.

His mother came in and noticed it at once.

Her face froze.

"What did you do?" she asked.

A-Shi told her everything — the river, the girl, the white stone.

His mother covered her face with her hand and cried very quietly, as though breaking something.

"Your grandfather chose you," she said. "He chose you by his last choice."

She lowered her hand at last.

"He has not left. He never left, ever."

A-Shi did not answer.

He held the bowl gently.

From that day, the bowl never lost its light. Even in deep winter it held a small faint glow that could not be extinguished.

He learned not to think of it as anything other than himself, forever — his and his wife's and his wife's child's children's — every descendant who would one day sleep beside that bowl.

Baiyin — she was not a person. She was the river. She was every river he had run past to reach this fire.

He understood it at once.

She had been the fire all along.

Not the bowl.

The world.

From then on, A-Shi was the keeper. He came in from the mill once a week, sat down beside the light, and fed it.

Every morning, every night, the fire burned beautifully.

He was not afraid anymore.
