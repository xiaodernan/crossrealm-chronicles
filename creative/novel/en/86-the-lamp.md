# The Unchanted Chronicle of Crossrealm: The Keeper of the Anchor Fire

**Genre**: Planar fantasy / dark epic / metafiction
**Adapted from**: Crossrealm Chronicles
**Volume XII**: The Long Journey — Fire and Ink

## Eighty-six: The Lamp

On Baiyin's last visit, the light in the house did not go out — not even for a moment.

Baiyin came in from the mountain, still cold from the north wind, still carrying pine ash on her sleeves. She sat down by the stove. She did not speak for a long time.

A-Shi watched her. She was doing the same thing she had been doing for six hundred years — sitting with the fire.

"Have you given it to them yet?" she asked.

He knew what she meant. The letter he had written to her, the one sealed inside the ledger — the one his grandfather had written through him in the last hour of the night.

"Almost," A-Shi said.

Baiyin nodded. Her face was calm. The fire lit something deep behind her eyes that A-Shi had never seen before — and he had not seen either since the night he woke up and she was sitting beside him.

"The ledger wants it now," she said.

A-Shi looked at the table. The ledger lay there, slightly open, its pages still warm from the stove. He had not closed it. He had not wanted to close it.

"When you give it to them," Baiyin said, "they will know you."

He did not ask who 'they' were. He already knew.

Six hundred years of keepers who had sat exactly here — who had kept this same stove — whose eyesight had faded into this same warm orange light, who had pressed their fingers to this same iron ledge, who had given their inheritance to this same fire.

Baiyin stood. She went to the doorway, stopped, turned.

"Tonight will be six hundred years from the first time a keeper stayed up too late waiting for the fire to speak," she said.

He was very still.

She was not smiling. She was looking at him the way a mother looks at a child before the child learns the lesson the mother learned at the same age, in the same kitchen, years ago — except Baiyin had lived through six hundred years of that watching.

"The ledger is nearly full," she said. "When it is full, the next keeper begins. It has always been so."

She reached the threshold.

"Goodnight, little one," she said.

She left.

After she was gone the stove leapt very high, higher than ever before, splashing orange across the ceiling and the walls. For one instant, every keeper's face was visible in the glow — six hundred years of faces, each one shaped by the same fire, each one lit by the same long vigil.

Then the flame settled.

A-Shi sat alone before the stove.

He unfolded the ledger and set it on the table. He opened the last page — already covered with his own hand, his grandfather's hand, Baiyin's hand, six hundred years of hands overlapping on the same sheet. He took the letter from beneath the stove's rim and set it on the ledger.

He closed his eyes.

The stove glowed hot but gentle in the dark.

When he opened his eyes, the letter was not on the table.

And the ledger's final page had gone blank — perfectly, serenely blank. Ready for whatever came next.

He knew what that meant.

He knew exactly what that meant.

The ledger, now empty, could accept the next thing — the thing he had not known he was waiting to write.

He settled back against the table's edge.

The stove-fire burned with a colour he had never seen in six hundred years — gold-white, clearer than any flame he had witnessed before — pure, ancient, enough to see all the way from the mountain.

The next day had not yet come.
