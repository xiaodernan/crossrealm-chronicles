## Ninety-nine: The Arrival

In the days after the gleam, the tree in the courtyard began to lean toward the ridge.

A-Shi noticed it slowly. The morning he first realized — one or two needles leaning ever so slightly eastward, toward the mountain — and the next day, dozens more — and within a week, all of them. The whole tree leaned. A compass fixed on a single direction.

He stood at the stove, staring through the window at the tree.

His mother noticed him staring.

"The fire-seed tree," she said after a while, "six hundred years old, bowing toward the ridge. What else could it be doing?"

A-Shi shook his head slowly.

She went outside to look at it up close.

A-Shi followed her out.

The tree was bowing — every needle angled eastward, toward the ridge where the greatest fire of all had once been kindled. The wind was blowing gently from the south, but not one needle wavered westward.

They were not bending under the wind. They were bowing toward the source — toward the place where the first spark had fallen from the sky, six hundred years ago, onto the broad white stone at the top of the ridge.

"She has remembered," his mother said quietly.

A-Shi did not ask who she was.

"The fire has always known," she said.

He understood.

The fire-seed tree had grown here for six hundred years because it was growing — toward the ridge where the fire had first arrived. Every generation of this tree had bowed toward the same ridge, toward the same white stone, toward the same moment when the first spark fell.

A-Shi stood with his hand on the tree's trunk.

The bark was warm.

Not warm from sunlight or from his own hand — just warm. As though the tree itself were lit from within, as though the memory of the first fire had been carried in its cells all the way across six hundred years of winters and summers.

"The fire did this," his mother said.

He turned to face her.

She nodded toward the tree.

"They have always been bowing," she said. "We just didn't see them until now."

A-Shi closed his eyes.

He could feel the tree's warmth through his palm — six hundred years of bowing toward the ridge, of remembering the light that had arrived here before any of them.

He opened his eyes.

"They stayed," he said.

"They always stay," his mother said. "Fire always stays."

The tree seemed to settle — to breathe.

A-Shi watched the courtyard go grey with dusk.

And far to the east, beyond the ridge, past six hundred years of waiting — the first sparks of the beacon that had never gone out met the horizon.
