## One Hundred and Thirty-six: The Third Bowl Comes Back

Wednesday of the nineteenth week after the spring equinox — afternoon — the sun high and bright.

A-Shi walked back with Aunt Guo — his mother said only: “Go and look.”

Aunt Guo’s stove was small — what she fed it was scavenged old wood — the fire burned steadily enough — but she said: “Mine doesn’t warm like yours.”

A-Shi said: “It’s the same fire.”

Aunt Guo shook her head — she fed it a dead branch — “Fires are different,” she said — “they know who is sitting at them.”

A-Shi stayed until dark — he said nothing — he didn’t go.

On the way home in the evening — he carried a small bag of dry rice — Aunt Guo had pressed it into his hands — she didn’t say anything.

His mother opened the door — saw the rice — nobody asked.

His mother poured the half-bag into the grain sieve — sifted it once — set it back in the jar.

“When you were small,” she said, “I would carry grain for you this way too.”

A-Shi said nothing.

The fire burned.

The fire burned.
