## Fifty-two: The Old Bowl

After 墨鹮 left, the house went quiet once more.

The bowl did not move from the table; nobody touched it. Every day A-Shi checked it once — just to see if it was still there. It was.

One evening the moon climbed s high the whole yard gleamed. A-Shi stood at the gate watching the old persimmon tree, then turned back to look at the bowl inside.

It reflected the moon; the moon reflected the bowl.

His mother called out. He went back inside.

She crossed the room and pointed at the bowl — no finger extended all the way, just a gesture toward it.

"This bowl," she said, "it was your grandfather's."

A-Shi raised his eyes.

"Which grandfather?" he asked.

"Lin Wen's father," his mother said. "He brought it down from the Yáohuǒ Station the year he visited the mountain passes."

Lin Wen did not add anything. He sat on the porch, his back against the doorframe, watching the moon.

His mother leaned toward him:

"When did Lin Wen's father bring it home?"

A-Shi thought for a moment. One memory surfaced immediately — when he was very young, someone had set a bowl down beside him while he was sick and asleep — then taken it away again. He had asked later who had been there. No one answered.

"The bowl…" his mother said, "is yours."

A-Shi blinked.

"…mine?"

She did not nod or shake her head — she just rested her hand on the bowl's shoulder.

A single bird crossed the sky, then silence again.

"The bowl," Lin Wen said from the doorway, "is not being given to you. It is being entrusted."

"For what?" A-Shi asked.

Lin Wen did not answer.

"You are its keeper," his mother said. "It will keep you."

No one moved. A-Shi stood in the middle of the room, face turned toward his mother; his mother's face turned toward the moon.

He stepped forward and was about to reach for the bowl — then paused.

His mother nodded slowly. Finally — just barely — he lifted the bowl with both hands.

The edge was smooth from many hands; the base held very faint scratches of old grain, hidden under layers of use.

He stood holding the bowl, feeling the weight of every house that had carried it before this one.

Lin Wen spoke one last line, so quiet that the moon seemed to listen:

"This bowl is our family."

A-Shi did not reply. He did not need to.

He set the bowl gently back in its place. He looked at his mother; he looked at Lin Wen; he looked out through the threshold at the dark persimmon tree in the moonlight.

He knew, now — that this bowl was not an heirloom — it was a beacon. It had never left anyone before.

The next day, he did not go out to the hills. He stood on the threshold until the moon went down. He touched the bowl from across the table once; it was warm.

He did not know why.

He just knew it was going to remain warm — because he was going to remain.
