## Thirty-seven: The Gate of Fire

On the second night, Lin Wen saw the wall at the North Pass.

He had walked for two days past the fourth pavilion. The path along it had gone pale — a stretch of earth where no grass would grow, dry as burned paper. At the end of it stood a wall of stone, so tall he could not see its top, reaching westward and eastward into the mountains. In the wall a single bronze gate, hung with centuries of green patina, and on the surface of the bronze —

dense strokes of handwriting, the same strokes that had filled the ledger, written in ash directly into the metal:

**火 — The Third Fire Waits.**

Lin Wen unstoppered the black lacquer box. He laid the three letters, one by one, against the gate's seam.

The first page — the leaf pressed beneath the folded blue scarf, taken from the old persimmon tree.

The second — the stroke of 火 from his father's ledger.

The third — the lampblack thumbprint mailed to him by 墨鹮.

Nothing happened for the first page. Nothing happened for the second. When the third touched the bronze, the gate gave a low groan, and a thousand years' worth of ash rolled from the cracks in the frame.

The gate opened.

There was no corridor inside. Only a long flight of grey stone stairs, leading upward and forward at once, and at the far end of the stairs stood one figure.

Grey-haired. Grey-robed. A long scar across his left cheek.

Lin Wen looked at him and could not speak. 墨鹮 — and yet every story he had ever been told gave Lin Wen one and the same face — the face of anyone who had been lit by fire for long enough to be called the fire's own.

"墨," was all he said.

Lin Wen managed one sentence. "I have no fire. I have only half of a pair of tongs."

墨鹮's mouth moved — the smallest motion that ever shaped itself into a smile.

"One who carries the tongs," he said, "is always prepared. The fire does not accept your hand — it accepts whoever has learned to trim the wick."

He extended his palm. In it sat a candle, its wax pale gold and heavy, lit from inside as if it had always been standing in a flame.

"You and I have met before," 墨 said. "In many other fires. This one was waiting."

Lin Wen took the candle. It lit up on its own when his palm closed around it.

Light passed over his face, over the ruined pavilions folded in the sack, over the ash drifting through the gate, over the soot shining black on the bronze.

墨 turned away.

Lin Wen did not call him back. He understood now what the old keeper had meant — the gate was not shut; it was simply... waiting. The fire would decide who would walk through it next.

Lin Wen made his way south with the candle held before him.

By the third pavilion, he finally understood his father: he had not lost the road. His father had simply carried the path for so long that it had become his — and the lamp he lit was never lit for himself.

On the day he returned to the Ember Post, the sky was full of rain, and no fire burned in the mountains.

He placed the candle on the altar-side wall of the loft, beside the oil lamp. The lamp steadied.

Lin Wen sat down in the middle of the floor, watched the flame, and waited for the next keeper.

The wind moved through the broken eaves and did not put out the flame.
