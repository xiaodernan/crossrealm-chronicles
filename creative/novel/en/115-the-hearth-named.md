## One Hundred and Fifteen: The Hearth Named

Ten days after the first day of spring, the yard grew ever warmer.

Half the needles on the fire-seed tree had turned a soft ivory yellow; every wind made them sway and hum. The ditch by the gate had turned to flowing mud. Moss had begun to creep out between the stones of the path.

His mother was cooking.

Xiao Huo crouched by the stove, watching the flame.

The old man had come again.

He had not brought any of the things he usually brought — no basket, no letter, no bundle of birch twigs. He had walked in wearing only a clean blue cotton robe, the color of a spring sky just deepening toward noon.

He said nothing as he came in. He only bowed his head once — a formal, old-fashioned bow that neither A-Shi nor Xiao Huo had ever seen him make before.

His mother smiled. "Come in."

He took a stool and sat beside the stove, facing it.

She brought out the meal she brought whenever a guest came — rice, cabbage tofu soup, preserved radish. Nothing unusual. Nothing added.

They filled the bowls high. There was a lot of rice. Only a little food.

The old man sniffed the air.

"Fragrant," he said.

A-Shi sat close to the stove, watching the fire. The fire swayed.

"Stove," he said.

The flame leapt once — then softened — then settled back into its long, low burning.

"It is spring," A-Shi's mother said. "Spring must have a name."

The kitchen went quiet.

"What name?" Xiao Huo asked.

His mother glanced at A-Shi.

A-Shi pointed at the stove. "Stove."

"Stove is not enough," the old man said.

From his robe he drew out a small clean piece of paper — no larger than his palm. He pressed one finger against it, leaving a faint soot print behind.

"炉," he said. "炉火."

"炉火," A-Shi's mother repeated slowly.

炉火.

After that day, no one in the house said "the stove" anymore.

They said: 炉火.

The mother said it first, the next morning, while stirring the porridge: "The stove-fire is coming warm."

Xiao Huo said: "The fire-fire is warmer today than yesterday."

A-Shi said: "炉火 breathes on its own at night."

Everyone left it at that.

The soup boiled a few minutes longer than usual.

The family gathered at the long table.

They ate.

In the iron box beneath the stove, there was a thin slip of paper that Bai Yin had written six hundred years ago. No one looked at it. They all knew what it was.

It was the name of the fire.

炉火.

In the first month of spring.

In this house.

Finally, it had a name.
