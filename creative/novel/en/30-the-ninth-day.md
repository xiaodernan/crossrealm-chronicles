## Thirty: The Ninth Day

On Double Ninth — the ninth day of the ninth lunar month, when Chinese families carry their elders up the hills to honor those who came before — his sister and her family came back from the south.

Lin Wen had been on the eastern slope that morning, gathering the old tree's fallen leaves to layer between fresh tea leaves. His mother was in the courtyard steaming Double Ninth cakes, the heat from the stove-mouth steaming her silver hair faintly silver-damp.

In the afternoon, his sister suggested they walk up to the slope. The boy rode on Lin Wen's shoulders the whole way, asking one question again and again:

"Will it bear this year?"

"Next year," Lin Wen said. "Maybe next year."

The new tree stood half a head taller than any of them by then. Six or seven fresh leaves were still pale green at the edges, and the older leaves had darkened to a dense shine. The red cord on the stake was scorched dark; the frame had not toppled; the scarf had not faded. His mother stood at the foot of the slope with her hands resting on her walking stick and looked at the tree for a long time. Then she said the words Lin Wen would carry to his own grave:

"My grandmother kept her mother's roots the same way."

His mother's mother had come from the back of the mountain. Her family had a persimmon tree whose fruit out-ripened every tree in the village. When the girl — Lin Wen's grandmother — married, she carried a small fistful of persimmon seeds away from the mountain in secret, without anyone knowing. She planted them on the new slope. The first tree died. The second lived. That was the old tree by the gate.

"My mother told me before she passed," his mother said. "A persimmon tree isn't poor. What it fears is that no one will plant the next one." She turned to look at him. "Seeing yours here — I know her generation has been answered."

The boy pressed his face against the frame, and touched each of the leaves, one by one. Lin Wen lifted him so he could see the underside of a leaf.

"Cool to the touch," the boy said. "Like your hand."

His mother sat down at the foot of the slope. His sister stepped behind him and steadied her mother's shoulders. The boy looked over and asked:

"Grandma, are you sitting here waiting for the new tree to bear?"

His mother laughed — the first real, unguarded laugh Lin Wen had heard from her in years.

In the evening, she took the clay bowl that had stood empty on the altar for years and polished it clean. From her pocket she drew one thing: a fresh-picked persimmon leaf. She set the leaf inside the bowl, centered it, and stepped back half a step.

"From now on, every Double Ninth," she said. "Whichever house has the new fruit, that year we remember."

The fire in the courtyard stove was lit. His mother sat in the chair at the center of the courtyard, with her mother's blue scarf laid across her knees. She said nothing more. There was nothing left that needed saying.

Lin Wen brought the Double Ninth cakes out. The boy held one out to Lin Wen and one toward his grandmother. His grandmother drew him up into her lap, and before anyone realized, he was asleep against her shoulder. The smell of ripe persimmon and cut chrysanthemum drifted from the courtyard gate, down the lane, and over the slope to where the new tree stood — drinking in the evening the way it drank in the rain.

On that Double Ninth, the old tree by the gate held twenty-three persimmons, and the new tree by the slope held none. The village neighbors, talking at the well, said:

"That family's trees — one of them is waiting for next year."
