## Seventy-seven: The Broken Stone

Seven days after the storm, A-Shi climbed the ridge again.

He did not climb to light the lamp. The lamp — six hundred years old — had gone out in lightning the other night. Today he had one single errand: to see the stone.

The snow had mostly thawed. The mud was soft. He carried a cloth bag over his shoulder and an empty iron canister in his hand.

The white stone stood alone at the summit.

A single crack split it clean from its crown to its root — the halves still holding their old shape, as if the stone had politely declined to fall apart, preferring only to open. A-Shi put his hand into the cleft.

It was cold inside — cold, and dense with ash and tiny chipped stones.

When he drew his hand out, he found something small and hard and round in his palm — like a coal.

He bent down slowly and picked it out.

Not just any coal — it was a kindling coal: the very first coal the first keeper ever carried up this mountain, six hundred years ago.

All this time — six hundred years — it had rested unburned in the heart of the white stone, kept warm by the beacon's never-ending vigil. The lightning struck the stone, scattered the glass, scattered the fire — but it could not break what had not been burning all this time. All it could do was let it fall — out of the stone's heart, and into A-Shi's open palm.

A-Shi stood holding the coal for a long time.

The wind came up behind him and ran through his hair, and he smelled old charcoal — not fresh fire but ancient ash — the smell that would have clung to the first keeper's sleeve the day he climbed up here, before anything in the valley had yet been born.

He opened the iron canister and laid the coal at the bottom. He shook some stove-ash out of the cloth bag and wrapped it around the coal, then sealed the canister with twine, several rounds, and tied it tight.

He did not hand it to anyone.

He carried it down the mountain himself.

When he reached home, he drew a small groove into the rim of his father's old workbench — not all the way through, only a shallow intent — and rested the canister inside, on a tiny flat stone, where the hearth's warmth could reach it.

His mother asked, as she always did:

"What is that?"

"Six hundred years," A-Shi said.

His mother looked at him a long moment. She did not ask again.

That night he woke to check the stove in a half-dream. At its very center there was a steady gold glow — and in that gold glow, something was moving, just barely perceptible, at the edge of the firelight.

The coal inside the canister had lit once more.

A-Shi closed his eyes.

The next morning the canister sat untouched in its groove. The stove burned as it always had.

Only one thing had changed: the fire-seed tree in the yard wore its highest needle in a deeper red than ever before — a red like a drop of fire that refused to fall.
