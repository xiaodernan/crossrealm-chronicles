## Forty-seven: The Black Letter

Three days after the spring equinox, a letter came up the mountain road.

The postman rode a scrawny mare up the valley and stopped at the courtyard gate. He held the letter out with both hands.

The envelope was black — black facing, black stamp, black corner. There was no address on it.

"This isn't a normal one," the postman said.

Lin Wen took the letter and tucked it into his coat.

"From Uncle Du," he said.

The postman nodded and rode away.

Inside the house, Lin Wen set the black letter on the clay bowl — half a candle was still burning there, its wick leaning in the light. He waited until dusk. He struck one match. He held it near the seal of the envelope.

The black paper began to show characters in dark soot against its darker grain.

**横桥。火等。**

Four characters in total.

Lin Wen pressed the match back into the candle and let it die out.

His mother was standing at the doorway. He did not turn.

"Your grandfather crossed there," she said quietly.

Lin Wen did not answer.

"There were two stone bridges across that ravine," his mother went on, "both built by the same family over two generations. Only one remains. The one with the carvings of 火 is still on the north end."

"Du the Elder," she said, "mentioned it once. He said that once your grandfather went across, only his line could be the next to cross."

Lin Wen set the envelope down. He gathered a few things — matches, a pouch of dried persimmon leaves, an old linen cloth, and a small cloth bag.

He took one old ribbon and one old fire-token. He set the token on the table, looking at it.

A-Shi came out of the house.

"Uncle," he said. "Where are you going?"

"横桥," Lin Wen said.

A-Shi did not ask any more questions. He put on his outer jacket.

"Then I come too," he said.

Lin Wen took the ribbon and put it around the boy's wrist. They walked north.

They stayed two nights in the Ember Post. On the third morning they remounted and headed further north. The stone slabs gave way to slabs broken in half. Then broken to gravel. Then gravel at the edges, mud, then thorn trees, then raw stones.

The bridge stood in front of them.

Only one plank of the eastern span remained. The far side of the bridge jutted out into nothing.

No one was there.

A-Shi looked slowly around.

"Uncle," he said, his voice small. "Everyone has gone."

Lin Wen shook his head slowly.

He stepped down from the horse. He opened the saddlebag, took out a small paper box, and tore it open. Inside were matches. The box looked burned but the matches were fine. Across the face of the box was printed:

**火**

Lin Wen turned it over. On the back was written, very faintly:

**你到了，火就来了。**

He tucked the matchbox into his pocket. He sat down on the bridge railing, his father's old knife lying across his knees. The wind was dry, and the bridge swayed a little under him.

A-Shi did not speak.

Then, somewhere beyond the far ridge, smoke rose straight up into the morning sky — a single column of white, moving steadily without bending. It was as though someone, very far away, was drawing a slow, infinite breath.

Lin Wen suddenly understood what Uncle Du had meant.

Uncle Du had not gone anywhere. He had been the gate all along.

The bridge was not supposed to be crossed. The bridge was supposed to be remembered. The next time someone stands on the far side of it, the fire will come out of the mountain.

No one said anything.

They did not cross the bridge.

The next morning, A-Shi woke with a blanket over him. He had not slept well; he remembered Lin Wen sitting on the railing the whole night.

He opened his eyes — and saw Lin Wen standing at the far edge of the bridge.

The wind was quiet.

A-Shi took one step forward. He stopped.

From somewhere beyond the ridge, there came a faint light — a pulse, almost, the color of a coal being uncovered from ash. It was far away. It was almost gone before he registered it.

Lin Wen turned around.

"火 is coming," he said.

He did not say from where. He did not say when.

A-Shi lowered his eyes and saw that Lin Wen's hand was closed around his palm, and the fire-token Lind Wen had brought was hot against his skin.

A-Shi realized then that Uncle Du had not been sending a letter about a place — he had been sending a signal. He had been waiting for them to come to where the fire was.

Lin Wen's father had divided that fire into three parts, once — once long ago, after the Spring Equinox. One part stayed in the old house, one part stayed with Uncle Du, one part had been walked north — and no one had said where it had gone.

Now he knew.

The grand-mother fire was still traveling. It had not burned out.

It had not stopped.
