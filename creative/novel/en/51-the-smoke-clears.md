## Fifty-one: The Smoke Clears

On a late-summer evening, a thin column of smoke lifted from the far ridge.

Straight up, never bending left or right — one pale thread drawn through the sky as it crossed the old pines, the roof of the house, the highest branch of the persimmon tree.

A-Shi was the first to see it. He shouted from the yard:

"墨鹮 is here!"

His mother froze holding the spatula. Lin Wen stopped too.

— a figure was walking across the yard toward them.

Grey-robed. Grey-haired. One scar across his left cheek. He was not walking along any path; he moved straight through the gateposts, as if the earth had agreed to let him through.

His mother stood on the threshold and said something — very low, very raw:

"Come in and sit down."

墨鹮 did not step in. He stopped in the center of the courtyard. He rolled his sleeves to the middle of his forearm — a gesture very like someone folding a scroll before opening it. There was a thin scent on him — faintly of persimmon leaves, slightly of ashes.

She did not feel it at first. She stepped forward; she placed a cup of water on the table. He did not drink.

"How long can you stay?" his mother asked.

墨鹮 said only:

"The fire waits for you."

One sentence. No more.

He turned to look at Lin Wen. Then at A-Shi. The look lasted a long time — long enough that A-Shi thought he might say something — the smallest warmest thing any of them had ever heard.

Then he turned and walked out of the yard.

A-Shi chased him to the gate. He wanted to ask something — but 墨鹮 did not look back.

Rain began to fall as he passed the ridge. A-Shi watched him crossing the hill, soaking each fold of his grey robe. He heard a voice — very faint, very distant — a whisper like tapped stones on the far side of some world.

A-Shi ran back to the house.

His mother was on the porch. She was trembling slightly, but she had not cried.

"He's gone?" A-Shi asked.

Lin Wen said: "He does not need an escort."

"Then the fire—is it out?"

"It isn't out," his mother said.

She looked at the clay bowl. She said nothing else for a long time.

"He was not the fire himself," she said at last. "He was the one who carried the torch from one hearth to the next."

That night the three of them sat around the bowl and said nothing. They did not speak of it, and they did not cry. They simply stayed beside the flame — for the rest of the year, and beyond.

From now on, there would be no more mysteries.

Only warmth — and the stubborn grace of not blowing it out.
