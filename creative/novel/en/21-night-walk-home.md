## Twenty-one: Night Walk Home

The mist on the morning he finally returned to the village had come sooner than usual. He could not tell whether it was mountain mist or dawn-mist, nor which year it was — perhaps not knowing was always a correct state. That, too, is narrative.

At the village entrance stood the oldest pomegranate tree where it had always stood. It had not changed; he had. He stood where he had stood many years before. He found that his heart was lower than most people's, lower still than any star's — and he sat with that for a long time at the edge of the road.

No one greeted him. This was normal — Lin Wen had been away for three years. The village had not decided whether it missed him at all.

He walked along to the little house at the eastern end of the village. The door had been nailed shut by his father, the nails driven deep into the wood so the door would never open again. When he reached it now, he found that the door could be pushed open.

It had not been rebuilt by his father. It had been rebuilt by the village children — a handful of village children carrying a sliver of dried ginkgo wood they had found deep in the hills, screwed it into the frame where the nails had been. No one asked why; no one needed to.

He stepped inside. The hearth was still lit.

The chairs had been replaced; the table had been replaced twice. But the hearth remained — the one his father had nursed the night he said the fire never stops was the reason we have somewhere to eat. That fire, that exact fire, was still burning there.

He sat in the doorway for a moment and listened to the fire. He heard three things: the fire was still burning, his heart was still warm, and somewhere in the back room, in a quiet corner of the kitchen, someone was boiling a bowl of soup.

He did not ask who. When he pushed the kitchen door open, he saw his brother. His brother lowered the ladle, turned only slightly, and held the bowl up for him.

"Once you finish it," his brother said, "you will no longer be the one who walked for three years. You will be the one who came home."

Lin Wen accepted the bowl. He swallowed the first bite. He suddenly remembered something from long before — the first morning he left the village, his mother had bound his waist with a red sash. He had been too busy walking to wipe his eyes, and no tears were ever recorded.

He found the sash. The next day, he put it on. As he tied the sash tighter, he stood straighter than he usually stood — because that was the same sash he had been wearing the first day he left home.

He looked at the hearth for a long time. Then he opened the door.

Outside, the village's elders and children were gathered. They seemed to have known he would be back. No one yelled across the yard; no one ran to embrace him. The village baskets were hung on the ginkgo tree by the hearth — precisely where his father had hung them three years ago.

After he swallowed his first bite, he heard something land inside the old clay pot by the hearth — a small folded slip of paper, signed in faded ink, bearing these words:

*Every house in this village has a door that can never be nailed shut. Walk your road; know that one door you cannot close may be a locked gate. But the locked gate—pushed hard enough — can still open.*

That was his father's handwriting, sure enough, as certain as any handwriting he had ever seen. But Lin Wen alone could read it — and that too is narrative.

He did not say anything. He smiled. He added dry firewood to the hearth, and the fire brightened.

He sat on the bench where he had sat as a boy. He closed his eyes.
