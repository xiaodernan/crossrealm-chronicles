## Ninety-seven: In the Light

A-Shi woke before dawn into a light that had no source.

He did not understand at first what was happening. The window was bright — but it was too early for the sun, and his mother had not yet risen to light more firewood.

It was coming from the stove.

The stove was glowing with a white light that rose through the floor, through the walls, through the wooden beams of the house, filling the kitchen with a radiance too steady and too clean to be any ordinary flame.

His mother stepped out of the back room and stood in the doorway.

She was barefoot. Her hair was still loose. She had not combed it yet. She looked like a woman who had been pulled out of bed by the fire itself.

She did not speak.

A-Shi followed her into the kitchen.

The light was too warm to be fire. It was warmer than any stove he had ever known.

She stepped forward and looked directly into the stove — the way she looked into it every morning, the way every keeper for six hundred years had looked into the stove they had been given to tend.

Then she knelt.

A-Shi knelt with her.

The stove's light filled the room entirely.

His mother's face was shining.

A-Shi looked at her face and understood, finally, what had always been happening in this kitchen — what had always been passed down — what had always been the reason six hundred years of women and men and children had woken before dawn and knelt before the stove and warmed their hands and said nothing.

What they had been doing was praying.

Six hundred years of prayers, spoken in silence, passed from hand to hand.

The stove had been answering every single one of them.

Now, at dawn, at the beginning of the seven-hundredth year, the stove was finally answering back.

White-gold light spilled out onto the floor, across the wood table, across their two faces — the first ray of the year — the first light of the year — no before the sun, but from the stove itself.

His mother put her hand out into the light.

She waited.

The stove did not burn her.

She put both hands into the light.

She wept — softly, without sound, without moving.

A-Shi did not know what she was seeing.

But he understood: she had been waiting for this her whole life.

He reached out his hand into the light beside hers.

The warmth came through his fingers — through his wrist — into his chest — and settled there, steady, like a heartbeat that had finally found its rhythm.

He stayed with her for a long time in that light.

Outside, the sky began to change.

The stove's light met the first real light of the morning coming through the eastern window.

Two lights met on the kitchen floor.

One did not cancel the other.

Both burned together.
