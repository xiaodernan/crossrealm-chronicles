## One Hundred and Two: The First Night

On the eighteenth day of the seven-hundredth year, Xiao Huo was given his first bundle of firewood.

He knelt in front of the stove the way he had seen A-Shi kneel a hundred times, and he stacked the logs one by one into the firebox, each one straight, none of them crooked. He did it with enormous care. The fire reached the tip of the first log and the tongue of flame twisted twice, three times, and then settled into a steady, confident glow.

His mother peeled garlic in the far corner of the kitchen. She did not turn around.

A-Shi sat on the stone step at the doorway with a porcelain teacup in his hands. The tea had gone cold. Every time Xiao Huo stacked a log, he looked up at A-Shi, and every time he did, A-Shi gave him one slow nod. After the third time, Xiao Huo stopped looking up, and A-Shi stopped nodding.

That evening, the first guest arrived.

A walker.

He had come from beyond the ridge — the same direction as the white stone. He carried a worn cloth bundle on his back, and on his feet a pair of grass-weave sandals worn so thin the soles had been worn through. When he reached the stove he stopped, looked at the fire for a long moment, then looked at A-Shi.

"Yours never goes out," he said.

A-Shi nodded.

The man said: "I heard someone say it once. I walked for a hundred and twenty-two days, just to see whether the stove really never goes out."

A-Shi did not speak. He poured the man a bowl of water.

The man took the bowl and stared at the water for a long time. The stove's reflection moved across the surface, trembling once with every rise of the flame.

"My mother used to tell me," he said at last, "that her mother used to tell her — and her mother used to tell her, from even further back — that a fire fell from the top of the white stone onto a stove in this house, into the hands of a girl with the surname Bai, six hundred years ago, and that it fell onto this house, and this courtyard, and this family. By my generation, my mother told me, hardly anyone believes the story any more."

He drank the water, then went on.

"I came to see for myself," he said. "Belief or no belief, it doesn't matter. Coming to look is enough."

A-Shi still did not speak. He fetched a chair and set it in front of the stove, then gestured for the man to sit.

His mother brought out rice and homestyle dishes — all of it made from pots warm from Xiao Huo's first fire. The garlic sizzled in hot oil in the next room. Outside, dusk came slowly. Xiao Huo sat on the edge of the stove, chin in his hands, watching the flames with wide eyes.

When the meal was done, the traveler reached into his bundle and drew out a small iron mirror.

The face of the mirror was browned black by centuries of fire. In its center, scratched into the metal, was a small flame drawn with a bit of twisted iron wire — precisely the same shape as the round little flame Xiao Huo had drawn on the kitchen wall.

"My mother asked me to bring this," the man said. "She said she did not know if you would take it."

His mother took the mirror, and her fingers touched the wire flame gently, once.

She was silent for a long time.

"Go home now," she said to the traveler. "The stove is here. There is a bowl by the fire if ever you come back through. When you reach your own house, feed one piece of wood into your own stove for the keeper of this one — you will be the third person in the seven-hundredth year whose own stove still holds a fire."

The traveler stood in the courtyard and bowed once — to the stove, to the tree, to the direction of the white stone.

As he walked away, the fire-seed tree in the yard swayed once in the night wind.

Xiao Huo began to cry.

No one knew why. A-Shi lifted him and held him tight, but Xiao Huo cried even harder against his chest. When the crying finally passed, he fell asleep in A-Shi's arms, still clutching the log he had not yet had the chance to put into the stove.

A-Shi set the log back in the woodpile. He did not feed it to the fire that night. He let the stove sleep, one night, unshaken.

Tomorrow morning, he decided, Xiao Huo would feed it himself.

That night, A-Shi wrote one more page into the box — longer by one paragraph than the hundred-and-one:

"When it was handed down to the seven-hundredth year, the fire was still burning. Not because no one had disbelieved — many had disbelieved. But no one ever let it go out. Belief does not have to survive for a fire to keep its ember. The fire does not require belief from anyone. It simply remains. Seven hundred years, one courtyard, one stove, one bowl of water offered with no words, bread shared without promise — and everyone who comes back to look is given a place that does not fall."

Xiao Huo slept deeply. His mother rubbed the little iron mirror clean and set it on the highest shelf above the stove.

The fire did not leap. It simply burned, low and steady, the way it always had.

On the night of the seven-hundredth year's first visitor, the stove burned one inch higher.
