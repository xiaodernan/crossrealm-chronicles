## Sixty-five: Still Burns

A-Shi was sixteen.

On the day of the winter solstice, his mother called him to the stove.

She brought out a small clay jar — as thick as a writing brush, big enough to hold a child's hand.

"You go now," she said.

"Where?"

"To deliver a letter."

"Who to?"

She didn't answer. She pressed the jar into his arms.

"Carry this with you."


A-Shi took the jar and set out. He climbed up to the ridge, to the broad white stone he had seen so many times.

Baiyin sat on the stone. She wore the same robe and white hair, but she seemed taller than before.

She smiled when she saw him.

"At last," she said, "you have brought fire."

A-Shi blinked.

She stood and took the jar from him. She opened it carefully.

Inside was a small, bright, living flame.

A-Shi didn't speak.

"This family," Baiyin said, "has kept fire for six hundred years."

"Six hundred years?" A-Shi asked.

Baiyin smiled.

"Six hundred years. My family came here to hold this mountainside and this hearth. They kept it alive through generation after generation."

"I see."

Baiyin nodded gently.

"Now you have been given it."

A-Shi looked at the flame inside the jar. It flickered and breathed, alive and warm.

"My family..." he began.

"They waited. Six hundred years."

"Six hundred years?"

A-Shi couldn't finish the word.

"They all kept it," she said. "Six hundred years. Every generation."

A-Shi stood in silence.

His family had lived under the same mountain six hundred years, and no one had ever let the fire go out.

He breathed deeply.

At last he found his voice.

"Unquenchable," he said.

Baiyin smiled.

He understood. From this night forward, A-Shi would keep the fire as every ancestor before him had kept it. He would be the one who made certain it never went dark. They belonged to each other.

Baiyin said nothing more.

She closed the jar and handed it to him.

Then she turned and walked away, disappearing into the pattern of light and shadow for good.

A-Shi stood in silence.

He made his way back home.

He approached the stove. He lit a small fire for himself.

He emerged from the kitchen.

His father was in the hall.

"Son," his father said. "Your mother sends you word."

"What?"

"Six hundred years."

A-Shi didn't reply.

Then his father said:

"This family will keep fire for as long as it stands. Forever. So long as we do it right."

A-Shi understood.

They held fire for six hundred years, and fire kept them for six hundred years.

This family would keep the same fire forever.

A-Shi looked out the window.

His stove flame still burned, brightly visible in his mind.

He would never let it die again.
