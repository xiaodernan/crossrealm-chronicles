## One Hundred and Eight: The Mountain Snow

On the last night of winter, a light snow fell on the courtyard.

Not much — just enough to whiten the stone steps, the white tablet at the gate, the stones before the stove. The needles of the fire-seed tree caught a thin film of it; when the wind stirred, some of the flakes shook loose and lay on the ground and did not melt.

By the next morning, the snow was still there.

Xiao Huo ran barefoot into the courtyard, crunching over the frost and squealing.

His mother was airing out the hearth-pots. She did not rush. She sifted the cold ash into a clay jar — the way she had done every year since the stove had been lit, the way Bai Yin had done it six hundred years ago.

There were no empty jars in the kitchen.

"Keep a pinch of last night's ash," she said. "That ash belongs to the ancestor-fire."

That night the stove burned higher than it ever had.

On the sixth day, another bundle of firewood appeared at the gate.

This time the slip of paper tucked beneath the cloth was empty except for one thin birch twig, tied with white thread.

A-Shi studied it for a long time.

"Here it is again," he said.

His mother knelt beside the bundle and looked at it. She did not untie it.

Xiao Huo came and stood beside her. He looked at it for a long time too. "The uncle is gone," he said. "This wood has no owner."

No one answered him.

A-Shi's mother stood, carried the bundle to the woodpile, and set it down beside the other nameless birch. Not next to it — behind it, ninth in line, the least visible of them all.

"Ninth," she said. "The least visible place."

A-Shi nodded.

Xiao Huo thought for a long moment.

"From now on, on the fifteenth of every month, we feed one branch to the stove," he said. "Whoever it belongs to — we do not ask."

A-Shi did not look at him. He nodded slowly.

His mother looked at the two of them for a long time.

Then she turned and walked into the kitchen and began to prepare the evening meal.

Another month passed.

In the spring, the first apricot blossom opened on the tree at the foot of the path. On the fourteenth day, they counted the wood at the hearth. Nothing had changed.

On the fifteenth night, the stove burned one inch higher than it had the night before.

And in the morning, a new slip of paper was tucked into the woodpile:

"The fifteenth. Again."

A-Shi read it twice. His mother read it once. Xiao Huo held the paper, turned it over, found nothing else, and put it carefully into the iron box.

The stove has not been asked to keep time.

It keeps it anyway — the fifteenth of every month, one inch higher.

No one has seen it happen.

And yet: every fifteenth the light over the gate stone seems just slightly warmer, as though someone unseen had spent the whole night watching the fire burn.

Xiao Huo asked A-Shi: "How does it know?"

A-Shi thought long before answering.

"Because someone is watching it on the fifteenth of every month," he said. "And fire always knows when someone is watching."

The stove.

Seven hundred years.

Not seen in six.

In the seventh hundredth year, it learned that it was being watched every month on the fifteenth.
