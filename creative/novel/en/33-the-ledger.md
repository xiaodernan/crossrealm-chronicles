## Thirty-three: The Ledger

Lin Wen read his father's unfinished manuscript on the night of the first snow.

The book was thin — a ledger from the small inn his father had kept down the south road. Everyone who needed a bowl of rice, a dry bed, a bench by the fire, passed through that village. Eighteen years' worth. The first ten pages were written in the careful squares of a schoolmaster's hand: date, name, place of origin, amount due. Clean rows, clean paper, nothing left behind.

After the tenth year, his father's hand changed.

From that year on, names disappeared from the ledger. He wrote no more names. Instead, he drew one character, over and over — 火, *fire* — a single character made of a 人, *person*, with two small strokes flanking it on either side, as if warmth had been added to the silhouette of a man. A lamp symbol.

Lin Wen turned the pages again and again across three nights. The first decade was exact; by the end, his father's entries filled one hundred and ninety-seven such stroke-counts of 火.

At first he assumed it was shorthand. Then he reached the last page — and stopped.

Just one stroke.

Just the first sweeping stroke of the character 火 — none of the rest of it ever arrived. No name. No place. No date. Only a single stroke, as if a lamp had been allowed to shine one last time before its light was cut by a closed hand.

His mother was standing in the doorway listening. The snow fell on the courtyard, on the old tree, on the straw ring at the base of the sapling — soundless.

She had heard him turning pages for three nights. Now she said, low and unmistakable:

"That stroke — it's yours."

It was the first time Lin Wen had ever seen his mother cry in the snow. There were no sounds, no tears run down; she simply stood with her back to the stove and her head bowed, the way a mountain remembers its own weight.

"You left by the rear door," she said. "I didn't see you go. Your father did." She paused. "He didn't call out. That morning, the belt your uncle had tied for you was still on your waist — he did not dare interfere with it. He only opened the ledger, and he made one single stroke."

She could not say any more.

Lin Wen lowered his head. Snow slipped from the cuff of his sleeve onto the book. He shielded it with his hand.

"Your father told me," she said at last, "that if this child one day comes back, there would be a second fire beside that first stroke. I did not believe it then. I believe it now."

Lin Wen sat looking at the single unfinished stroke on the page. He understood suddenly what his father had been writing all those years — not a ledger of debts or prices at all. His father had been keeping an account of every person who had come to that road and had left it warm. The price of rice, the cost of straw, the wages owed to porters — none of it truly mattered to him. What mattered was that when someone left, the house was still warm for the next traveler.

His father had kept one true book of accounts his entire life. The total was 火. Every person who had passed through had been remembered by a stroke of it.

He no longer wanted to close the book. He understood now what it had been waiting for all these years.

He turned to the last empty page. He lifted his pen.

He wrote a single character:

*火。*

Fire.

The stove in the kitchen cracked once, deep in its iron belly. His mother turned her face away, wiping her eyes with the back of her hand. Outside the snow had stopped. A clean, gold moon climbed the ridge. The bare branches of the old tree gathered the light the same way his mother's hands had folded the blue scarf into the clay bowl — as if the whole earth understood what it was holding, and chose not to break anything.

Lin Wen sat very still. He knew his father had been watching over him all these years.

He knew now that a whole life could begin — could always begin — from a single stroke of 火.
