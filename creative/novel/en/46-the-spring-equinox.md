## Forty-six: The Spring Equinox

On the day of the spring equinox, the mountain peach bloomed for the second time.

A-Shi rode down the eastern slope on a jujube branch — his own family's old jujube tree had died decades earlier; what was left of it served as a boy's toy now. He followed the white-stone wall down to the valley floor, where the rice paddies were just about to fill with water.

At the gate stood an old man with bones slightly bent, his skin folded like a cured walnut shell.

"You must be Lin Wen's boy," the old man said.

A-Shi nodded.

"Come inside," Uncle Du said.

A-Shi set down the bundle he carried at his side of the doorway: a single wax stick, a pouch of dried persimmon leaves, a small ceramic bowl, and one box of matches.

"These are for you," he said.

Uncle Du looked at the bundle without touching it.

After a long moment, he invited A-Shi in. The courtyard was bare; the sky above it was already a wide orange-blue; the far ridge breeze carried the smell of scorched cedar.

Du took the bundle inside the kitchen. He lifted the lid of the empty stove — cold. He turned to the boy.

"This stove hasn't been lit for three winters," he said.

A-Shi did not answer.

Du took out a small cloth bundle from under the floorboards, and laid it on the table. Inside were two things: an iron fire-token, black with age, its rim perfectly smooth; and a short stub of candle wax, deep amber still.

"A friend once mailed this to me," he said nodding toward the table. "'Both for you.'"

He paused. "You must take these back now."

A-Shi shook his head.

"Uncle Du," he said, "these are yours. I didn't come to collect them."

Du closed his hands over A-Shi's sleeve. He gave him the iron token.

"Then remember this," he said, "and when the time comes — you will know."

They lit the stove together.

A-Shi struck one match. He held the flame to a dry persimmon leaf. The first curled instantly, the rest followed in an orange thread that rose and lifted the whole room.

Uncle Du put the blue bowl he had kept on the shelf into the light of the flame.

"Lin Wen does not visit anymore," he said. "But he sends us light that carries a person — and carries a name."

After the fire had steadied, A-Shi returned to the eastern slope. Du knelt at the gate and pressed a bowl of rice into his hands.

"Take it home," he said.

A-Shi walked with the bowl against his chest the whole way.

By the time he set foot upon the eastern slope, the whole mountainside had gone into dusk. The stove behind him in Uncle Du's courtyard was lit — and over every roof in the valley, each flame that had lived through the winter quietly glowed on.

One day, he thought — every house in the village will have one and the same light. And no one will ever remember who delivered it.

— but the fire remembers.
