## Thirty-two: The Start of Winter

On the first day of winter, the persimmon tree by the gate dropped all of its leaves.

A whole tree stores a year of light inside its leaves, and when they fell, the courtyard was covered in yellow — a yellow that could not be swept away. His sister and the boy were leaving that morning. The boy tucked two fallen leaves beneath the car's rear window — he said the winter had to travel back with them. His mother watched the boy's small hands. She did not tell him to put them back.

She stood in the courtyard for a long time. Someone to the north was burning straw. The smoke came drifting in, and she stood there among the fallen leaves and thought: this is what a life is, when you have lived it. Sowed in spring. Stood in summer. Held in autumn. Put away in winter. A fallen leaf is not an ending; it is everything that was folded into the year, lying where it landed.

The leaves of the new tree fell too. It held on the longest. On the night of the first frost it had kept its leaves, and on the first day of winter it loosened the last two — they sank to the dark soil at the base of the frame and lay like a small green lamp.

His mother went into the house and brought out a bundle of rice straw. She came back and wove a loose ring of it around the base of the frame. She did not wrap it tight — only a loose circle, wide enough for the wind to pass through. It was her answer to the old tree's winter, simplified into a sapling's terms. The old tree is a whole tree; the young one has not grown into its own yet. The ring of straw had to have holes for the wind to breathe through.

She finished. She clapped her hands once.

"Done."

"What's done?"

She looked at the old tree. Then at the slope. Then at her son.

"The tree is done," she said. "The house is done." She paused. "I'm done."

Lin Wen looked at her — the skin on her hands like bark, the earth still under her nails. Those hands had carried his whole childhood. He wanted to say: Mother, you have years left. You have more to teach me. But the light in her eyes burned brighter than gold, and he could not find his voice.

She did not cry either. She simply opened her hands and looked at them, and said:

"You went far, and you saw everything. When you came back — you've written it all down, haven't you?"

Lin Wen nodded.

"Then write it down," she said. "The book your father left — it's still sitting at the bottom of the chest. I never let myself throw it out."

Lin Wen knew the book. His father had been a bookkeeper — the village accountant. In the last season of his life, he had left a manuscript that was neither accounts nor poems. Lin Wen's mother had kept it sealed for years. It was her husband's heart; it was the house's secret.

Today, she brought it out.

"It should have been yours a long time ago," she said. "You read better than I do. Finish it."

That night, when Lin Wen opened the manuscript, he found a slip of paper tucked between the first two pages. His father's handwriting:

*I couldn't finish it — no one was here yet who could carry it further.*

Lin Wen laid the slip on the table. He set the manuscript straight. He pulled clean paper out of the drawer, uncapped the pen, and opened the window. The first snow of the year was falling — onto the bare upper branches of the old tree, onto the straw ring at the foot of the new tree, onto the flat stones of the gate path. Every falling thing fell where it belonged.

He gripped the pen and wrote the first line.
