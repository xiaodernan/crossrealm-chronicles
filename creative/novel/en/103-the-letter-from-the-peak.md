## One Hundred and Three: The Letter from the Peak

Nineteen days after the guest had gone, another one arrived.

He did not speak. He bowed once toward the direction of the white stone, handed a letter to A-Shi's mother, and stepped back three paces — no more, no less.

He wore a white hemp robe that had been washed many times, its cuffs frayed thin from use. At his waist hung a small copper mirror, polished to a mirror's shine so that his face above it looked paper-white. The mud on his sandals had dried and re-wetted so many times it had become a layered crust.

His mother took the letter.

She did not open it.

A-Shi recognized the way it had been sealed — the sealing-wax carried the mark of a small flame. That kind of mark belonged only to things that lived by a stove.

His mother invited him in and set a bowl of water before him. He would not drink it. He said he could not drink — he kept the watch on the mountain peak, had come down only to deliver this letter, and would return immediately once it was delivered.

"Delivered where?" his mother asked.

"To those who wait below," he said. "I was told only to bring it."

A-Shi's mother did not press. She rose and went to the stove.

The man left without speaking another word, and he walked away quickly — as though the mountain above him were waiting with something far more urgent than water and a chair.

The courtyard was very quiet after he was gone.

The fire-seed tree stirred in the wind.

A-Shi's mother sat down at the stove with the letter in her lap. She glanced at Xiao Huo, then at A-Shi, and with a small knife she broke the seal.

Inside was a single page, folded with the same clean precision with which logs were stacked.

She unfolded it and read.

Her eyes were wet before she had finished the first line.

A-Shi stood behind her and read over her shoulder.

Xiao Huo climbed up onto a bench and read too — or pretended to. He could not yet read well, but the shape of the writing was enough.

The characters were thin and upright — exactly like the handwriting of the first keeper.

His mother read it twice.

She passed the page to A-Shi.

It was short. One line:

"You who keep the flame below — it is still burning there. We who keep this flame above — it is still burning here. Leave one sentence for whoever comes after — fire is not kept for one person. It is kept for everyone who might still find the way back."

No signature followed.

Only one mark — the same small flame pressed into the wax.

In the courtyard, the fire-seed tree trembled once — all of it, from root to tip — and then was still again.

Xiao Huo finally asked: "Is there a stove on the peak too?"

His mother nodded.

"When the fire first fell from the white stone seven hundred years ago," she said, "it fell into a stove. The first one to catch it was Bai Yin — but Bai Yin was not the only one keeping watch. There have always been others. Up on the peak, in a small stone house beside the white rock, someone has been keeping the ancestor-flame for seven hundred years."

"Who is the last one?" Xiao Huo asked.

"No one knows," she said. "Their family has passed it down for seven hundred years. Bai Yin's line went up into the mountains before anyone thought to ask. They call the flame they keep up there the ancestor-flame. Six hundred years, no word came from them. And now —"

She lifted the page.

"— they have written to us at last."

A-Shi turned the page over.

On the back, one more line:

"Autumn equinox of the seven-hundredth year — the ancestor-flame on the peak will be fed once. Feed your stove the same wood this year too — white birch."

A-Shi looked at that line for a long time.

Outside, the fire-seed tree swayed in the wind.

Xiao Huo pressed his small hand against his mother's sleeve.

That evening, A-Shi walked out alone and came back carrying one piece of white birch wood.

White birch does not grow near this house — it grows on the northern slope above the snow-line, close to the white stone. There was only one piece of white birch in this house, and it had been stored away by Bai Yin herself, six hundred years ago. It was so dry and so old it felt like iron in A-Shi's hands.

His mother cut the log in half.

One half went into the stove.

The other half she wrapped in an old cloth and placed beneath the iron box.

"This half," she said, "will wait for Xiao Huo. When he is old enough to walk up the mountain, he will carry it himself."

The fire lit immediately.

The whole kitchen brimmed with white-gold light.

Outside, the fire-seed tree leaned gently in the wind, its needles sighing — like someone reading one very old, very long, very patient letter that nobody in this house had opened for seven hundred years.
