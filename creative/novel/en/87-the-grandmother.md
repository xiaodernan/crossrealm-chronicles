## Eighty-seven: The Grandmother

On the third morning after Baiyin left, A-Shi found something on the kitchen table he had never noticed before — a folded square of old cloth, thin as a letter, wrapped with string that had gone brown with age.

He opened it slowly.

Inside, a single strip of paper remained — most of it had turned to dust where the flames had brushed it, but the characters across the top held firm:

Baiyin's hand.

Three characters.

Bai Nainai — Grandmother Bai.

A-Shi stared at them until the stove leapt, once, behind him.

He turned. The flame burned steady and patient, gold as always — but taller than before. Six hundred years of keepers had tended this same fire, and none of them had understood what it was. Or they had, and none had been able to say it aloud.

A-Shi reached behind him and touched the stove's edge. Warm, as it always was.

That was when it came to him — all six hundred years at once, a lifetime he had not lived but had inherited — every keeper's name in the ledger, every story about the fire that never went out, every generation of families who had called that stove their grandmother:

The fire was the family.

His grandfather's grandfather's grandfather — the one who lit the first stove — his father's father, his father, himself — all of them wearing the same kitchen's firelight, all of them warmed by the same long table:

Grandmother Bai — the one who never spoke, the one whose name appeared on no ledger, the one who never aged, the one who never left the stove.

Baiyin's strip was nearly gone. On the very end, in the same faded ink, one more line — small enough to forget, large enough to tell the whole truth:

The fire is Grandmother. Grandmother is the fire.

A-Shi did not move for a long time.

His mother came in with soup. She looked at the cloth in his hand, at the tiny strip of paper, at his face — and knew.

She sat down across from him.

"Six hundred years," A-Shi said.

"Six hundred years," she said.

Neither of them said any more.

The stove rose steady and gold. The yard snow had nearly stopped; a thin sun edged through the window and touched the table — touching the ledger — touching the strip of cloth — touching everything A-Shi had ever been or ever would be.

Baiyin's question returned to him suddenly: Have you given it to them yet?

He had.

They had answered him.

He closed his eyes and bowed his head. Six hundred years of silence bowed with him.

The stove flared once — and settled low, content — a grandmother tucking in her grandson for the night.
