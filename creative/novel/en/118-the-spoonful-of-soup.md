## One Hundred and Eighteen: The Spoonful of Soup

The rain ended for good seven days ago.

Old Li came every day for those seven days. On the sixth day he laid a small cloth bundle on the stove. His mother opened it and found a white patch of fabric — a faint flame drawn on it, stitched very finely along its edges.

He said nothing, and left.

On the seventh day he came again. The moment he stepped inside he went straight to the stove, picked up an old wooden spoon, and ladled one spoonful of hot broth from the pot and drank it.

His mother watched his hands on the spoon.

"I've come to learn how to feed the fire," he said.

His mother stood still.

"I don't want a title," he said.

A-Shi came out of the kitchen with a bowl of rice.

"My six family members haven't eaten," he said.

His mother smiled.

"Anyone who comes in from the rain," she said, "doesn't need to ask to be fed."

Old Li stared at the stove.

"Seven hundred years," he said slowly. "I've been looking at the wrong fire this whole time. These days I'm finally beginning to see it properly."

"It breathes," he said.

A-Shi stepped back.

In the yard the fire-seed tree shook gently. The third flush of new leaves was opening.

Seven hundred years of keepers in this house — the stove, the kitchen, this room. No one invited anyone else inside the gate. It was a quiet rule the household kept without speaking aloud.

A-Shi watched the fire. His mother tended the stove. This house had always been a Hearthkeeper's house.

Today, for the first and maybe the last time in seven hundred years, another hearth-hand would join them at the fire.

Old Li did not take the spoon back. He let it rest in the pot.

Fire.

He placed the spoon back in the pot and his hands stayed in the glare of the flame a moment longer than necessary.

"Tomorrow," A-Shi's mother said. "Tomorrow you'll come."

Old Li did not say anything. He only nodded.

He left, walking back down the lane in the rain-fresh air.

A-Shi and his mother and Xiao Huo stood at the porch for a long time watching him go.

The fire-seed tree swayed.

The stove burned very steadily.

They had six bowls tonight.

They had always cooked six bowls.

That night in this house, the fire was very bright.

All the trees in the yard, all the stones, all the ash in the stove, all the water on the path — they were all a little warmer than any previous night this year.

The stove had not gone out.

The whole yard had not gone out.

Spring had come.
