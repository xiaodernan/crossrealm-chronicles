## Thirty-eight: The Road Home

Lin Wen came back to the old house before the end of May.

It was the day of Full Grain in Ear — the same season of wet green he had left in. The barley on both sides of the road was shoulder-high, the wheat turning the first yellow of summer. He did not ask for directions. Each pavilion along the route had already drawn the map he needed. The box on his back was lighter than it had been on the day he left, by exactly the weight he had never known it carried.

He did not go inside at first. He walked to the eastern slope instead.

The sapling stood three full feet tall now and had pushed out twelve fresh leaves, new green shading into full emerald. The red cord his mother had tied to the frame at midsummer the year before was weathered but still held fast. Sometime in the season, one corner of the blue scarf had loosened in the wind — he stepped forward and pressed it flat again. He was beginning to understand what it means to carry the light for someone who cannot yet carry it themselves.

The old persimmon tree had set two fruit this year.

Only two. The tree was growing old, and the harvest had been thinning for several seasons now. The two persimmons hung on the branch nearest the gate. He had seen them from the road.

He knocked on the door.

His mother did not answer at once. The stove-blow collapsed with a low cough. Then she was there in the doorway — apron tied high, the bottom of the pot still sooty from last night's dinner. She looked at him for a long time.

Then she said:

"You came back."

Lin Wen nodded.

"What is that?" she asked.

He lifted the black lacquer box off his shoulder and opened it. He took the candle — the one he had been given at the North Pass and never once allowed to go out — and laid it in his mother's hands.

She lowered her head and held it. Her eyes reddened.

"Your father," she said, "every year, I watched him blow out candle after candle. I never thought one of them would be brought home alive."

She went to the cupboard and took out a new clay bowl — plain brown clay, no glaze, the same shape as the old one but a little wider — and set the candle inside it. Beside it she laid the persimmon leaf, the folded blue scarf, the bark, and the three letters, side by side. The bowl held a low pulse of warm water-like light that rose and fell with the slow rhythm of the flame.

"Your father never came back," she said. "But you did."

Lin Wen nodded.

That evening, after they had cleared the table, Lin Wen drew the iron token from his pocket — he had meant to send it back north, to return it to the stone recess where he had found it. Instead he quietly placed it beside an old photograph tucked at the back of the shelf — a photograph his mother had always kept of his father as a young man. He set the token there, so the iron and the photograph faced the room together.

His mother brought the pot of rice out. Lin Wen noticed that she was not looking at him. She was watching the candle in the new clay bowl — its flame standing steady and straight, undisturbed by wind or draft or passing shadow.

The fire was steady.

— like the first fruit that had hung on the persimmon tree in the year he was born.

The fire was steady.

— like his father had said, quietly, more than once: where the fire doesn't die, the home doesn't unravel.

— like the whole spring had come and gone and the persimmon tree had not dropped a single leaf in the season, and his mother had not been able to look at any of it, and she had held it all anyway.

Lin Wen sat with the bowl in his lap and could not speak, and his mother did not ask him whether he was crying or only tired. She simply sat beside him, ate beside him, and stayed beside him until the moon turned.

Full Grain in Ear — the day the year's weight tilts sweet toward summer — lasted all the way to morning. Neither of them went outside.
