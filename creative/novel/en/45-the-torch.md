## Forty-five: The Torch

On the day of the Little New Year, his sister's family came to visit.

A-Shi ran out to the gate to greet them. This year he was twelve years old, small still, with broad round cheeks and an old cloth bag slung across his shoulder.

Inside the bag was a clay bowl — one he had made himself in a kiln out on the hill. The rim was slightly crooked, and there was a fine crack along the bottom, but it held water.

"Uncle," he called to Lin Wen, "look at my bowl."

Lin Wen took the bowl and studied it. He ran one thumb along the crooked edge.

"Did you make it?" he asked.

A-Shi nodded.

His mother heard them from the kitchen. She said nothing. She only brought out a small old box.

Inside the box was a piece of wax — the last remnant of the candle his father had left behind. Lin Wen had not seen it in months.

His mother placed the box in her son's hands.

"Divide this onto the table," she said.

Lin Wen took the box. He could not find his voice. He suddenly understood what this was — the fire his mother had been holding in trust for him all his life.

During dinner, everyone called out the family greetings, and the house shook with laughter. It had been a long time since they had all sat under one roof.

After dinner, Lin Wen rose and placed A-Shi's bowl in the center of the table. He set a fresh candle inside it and struck a match. The flame rose.

His mother went silent, then quietly spoke two words — Lin Wen knew these were the first two characters of his father's name. She never finished the third.

No one spoke after that.

A-Shi sat beside the grown-ups without speaking, watching the flame inside the bowl.

"A-Shi," his mother said at last, "come tomorrow. I have something for you."

The next morning, he came. His mother gave him a small bundle wrapped in cloth.

Inside were three things: a box of matches, a sliver of that same wax remnant, and one persimmon leaf.

"When you are back at home," she said, "light the candle stub in your own stove. Then let it burn down. That fire will then belong to your family."

A-Shi looked down at the bundle. He had no way of saying whether or not he understood. He simply nodded.

On the second day of the month, he wore the stub of wax inside his coat and walked home along the mountain road. The snow was firm underfoot.

At home he knelt before the stove and struck the match. The flame leapt. He breathed on it very gently — his breath was warm and slow.

His grandmother woke in the back room. She thought it was already morning; she could not see the stove.

What she heard was a kettle being set on to boil.

"That's the light," his grandmother said to herself, "of someone else's child."

A-Shi did not answer. He only lowered the bowl over the flame — the same way he had been taught.

The next spring he returned to the old house. He came carrying the same small clay bowl in both hands, the flame still burning in it.

"This is mine," he said. "Just like yours."

His mother took the bowl and held it in her lap. She sat for a long time. She reached out and lit the candle on the nearest shelf — an old one she had been keeping.

She wept very quietly without making a sound.

— never before in all their lives had anyone brought out a bowl that held the same bright fire —

— given to the third generation.
