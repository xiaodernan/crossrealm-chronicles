## Ninety-four: The Mother's Stove

When the light in the kitchen began to change, the mother was already awake.

A-Shi came down the stairs carrying a bowl of his own and stopped at the threshold.

She was on her knees in front of the stove.

She did not turn around when she heard him.

The stove burned low and gold, as it always burned in the morning, before anyone else was awake to see it.

His mother had a small wooden bucket in one hand and a bundle of dry rice-husk in the other. She tipped a little of the husk into her palm, and let it flicker into the stove.

A wreath of incense lifted from the stove.

"For?" A-Shi asked.

His mother did not turn around.

"Grandmother Bai," she said.

He did not ask which grandmother.

They were on the same ground now. Six hundred years had gone through their house — generations of keepers, each of whom had sat right here in front of this stove, each of whom had known his mother's mother and her mother's mother before her.

A-Shi thought about six hundred years: all the meals shared, all the winters, all the nights when nobody slept before dawn. He thought about the names he had only heard from his grandfather, who had only heard them from his grandfather — names of people who had sat right here, smelling exactly what she was smelling now, feeling what she was feeling now.

He understood, suddenly, why his mother woke so early every day.

She lit this stove before anyone else's eyes were open so it would be burning before anyone else needed it — not for them, but for the ones who came before.

The stove was still warm from yesterday.

His mother set the bucket back on the shelf where it always lived.

"Your grandfather," she said, without turning, "lit this stove every morning for forty years."

A-Shi knew it.

"I was born after the third year of the sixth hundred years," she said.

He did not know she remembered everything so clearly.

"The house has always been the same house," she said. "The stove has always been the same stove. The grandmother has always been the same grandmother."

The stove gave one small, contented crack.

Outside, the sky brightened a little more.

A-Shi sat down on the stool where he always ate.

His mother finally turned around and looked at him.

She smiled very faintly.

"Six hundred years," she said.

He nodded.

"Six hundred years," he repeated.

They did not look at each other directly, either of them.

The stove burned steadily, steady as it always had been, as it always would be.
