## Seventy-five: The Character

Early summer arrived at last. The fire-seed tree in the yard pushed out its fifth needle.

Every morning A-Shi watered it with one spoonful of stove-ash slurry. Every morning he tended the stove. And every morning, after these two things, he took a sheet of trimmed coarse paper from the kitchen stack and wrote one character.

The character was 记 — to remember.

His strokes were ugly. The dot slanted. The horizontal stroke bent in the middle. The hook dragged on too long. Baiyin had taught him: the brush must already know what the heart knows, before the stroke begins.

"Fire cannot write the character itself," Baiyin said. "Fire needs somebody to write it for them."

"Write it for whom?"

"For everyone," she said.

Then she reached into her sleeve and brought out the same thin ledger — the one in which every keeper before him had, on their final night, set down one character. She turned it to the last page.

That page was blank.

Not blank — unfilled.

Baiyin laid the ledger on the hearthside, open, like an offering.

"Six hundred years," she said. "Every keeper before your time, on their final night, set down one character 记. One hundred and sixty-seven keepers, one hundred and sixty-seven characters. One page remains — and it is yours."

A-Shi stared at the empty page.

It was vast — big enough to hold anything the world had to say.

He found he could not write.

Baiyin did not press him. She sat beside him, watching the fire, and waited.

After a long silence the stove leapt once — a small, bright tongue — and settled.

"You do not have to write it today," she said. "You are learning. When you are ready, you will be ready."

A-Shi nodded.

From that day, he wrote the character 记 every day.

One month in, his strokes grew regular. Three months in, the brush felt heavy in his hand — heavier than a kitchen brush should — as though his grandmother's fingers were wrapped around his knuckles, as though his great-great-grandmother's bones were settled along the shaft.

Two months after that, a familiar scent drifted off the stove — scorched cedar, like old paper catching fire. He looked up: the fire-seed tree in the yard shivered gently, though no wind was blowing.

Baiyin came again the next morning.

She held his practice sheet up to the light and read it for a long while. Then she nodded.

"You may write it now," she said.

"Now?"

"When you feel ready," she said. "Not today — one day."

She folded the ledger and set it in his hands.

"The six hundred and second keeper," she said, "begins with this page."

A-Shi looked down at the ledger.

He understood at last: the day he wrote that final 记, the ledger would close. Six hundred and two keepers, six hundred and two characters — and on the far side of that word, the world would look different than it had looked for six hundred years.

He laid the ledger back by the stove, and turned to Baiyin.

"When the tree is grown," he said.

Baiyin nodded.

The stove burned steadily. Out in the yard, the fire-seed tree grew upward, quietly, red at the tips — six hundred years of fire, coming home to rest.
