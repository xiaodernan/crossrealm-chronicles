## 8 · Winter solstice (24 solar terms · solstice · 冬至)

- The winter solstice is one of the Twenty-Four Solar Terms: the Sun reaches ecliptic longitude 270° — the longest night of the year in the Northern Hemisphere (near zero sunlight inside the Arctic Circle). It falls, historically, on December 21 or 22.
- In Chinese tradition the solstice meant "一阳生" — the rebirth of yang from the longest yin of the year. 《礼记·月令》 prescribes the rites: the winter solstice rite honors heaven, the rite of the stove follows; the stove is the protector of the family diet.
- Modern Chinese families still eat dumplings on winter solstice today — "冬至不端饺子碗，冻掉耳朵没人管" — and households across southern China and Yunnan keep the firepit burning all night on solstice ("火塘不过夜", the firepit must not go out at night; the family elders keep the ember alive).
- Chinese emperors since the Han dynasty held the "grand solstice ritual" at the southern suburban altar of the capital — the worship of heaven at the longest night.
- Kyoto, Japan — the kamado no kami （竈神）, the stove god, is not extinct; the Gion district still keeps the nightly fire for the kamado observance today.
- Spain's Las Fallas, Turkey's Nevruz — solstice fire is a Eurasian thread that ran from Caucasus steppe nomads through Anatolia and into the Balkans — fire is the thing every one of these holidays uses to mean "the year turns".
- Crossrealm mapping (canon analogy, not story fact): Volume 112 writes the solstice as "a day with no day" — the family keeps one ember in the stove all night — a Chinese folk precedent with roots from Han through the modern rural villages; the invention in the story is Xiao Huo's individual responsibility, not the rite itself.

## 9 · Eternal flames — empirical samples

- **Ise Grand Shrine, Japan** — every 20 years the Inner and Outer Shrines are fully reconstructed (Shikinen Sengū); the latest (62nd) was completed in 2024. The fire at the new shrine is ceremonially fed from the previous shrine's ever-burning flame — a fire more than 1,300 years old.
- **Paris, France** — the Arc de Triomphe's Tomb of the Unknown Soldier: its flame has burned continuously since November 11, 1923 — 102 years; it is re-lit every evening at 6:30 by the association La Flamme de l'Espérance armed with the censer kept inside the tomb itself.
- **Moscow** — the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier on the Kremlin Wall, Moscow, has burned without interruption since 1967; its flame is renewed annually on 22 June (the Nazi invasion of 1941).
- **Tokyo, Ise Jingu** — the Shikinen Sengu completion in 2024 was the 62nd reconstruction since AD 690.
- **Hiroshima Peace Memorial Park** — the peace flame lit in 1964 has not been extinguished since; it will not be extinguished until the last nuclear weapon in existence is destroyed.
- **Moscow, London** — the Eternal Flame at Buckingham Gate; the Chapel of the Nine in the Kremlin.
- Crossrealm mapping (canon analogy, not story fact): Bai Yin's "600 years of white birch" and the ancestor-fire's "600-year hearth" are not documentation — they are a practical instantiation of the same architectural instinct that built Ise Jingu's repeated reconstruction. The novel transplants that instinct into a single Chinese stove and a single family.

## 10 · Fire as household identity (folklore and the hearth)

- In premodern Russia, on **Annunciation** and other feast days, the household fire was treated as part of the ancestral lineage — the fire was lit in the great hall and not moved until the feast was over; the Tsar's court maintained its own "royal fire" in the Kremlin.
- In the **Oural** (the Ural Mountains) there were entire villages where the hearth of the founding family was kept burning for generations; the phrase "огонь не угасает, дом живёт" — "the fire does not go out, the house lives" — is still heard in some Uralian villages.
- In **Japan, the kamado kami （竈神）** is honored from the fifteenth to the nineteenth of December every year in Kyoto; the tradition is described in the Nihon Shoki (AD 720) and is still practiced without a break.
- In **France, the Veneration of the Eternal Flame** — the fire in the tomb of the unknown soldier was ceremonially rekindled in 1923, but the Saint-Chapelle in Paris maintained a liturgical flame since the Middle Ages.
- Crossrealm mapping (canon analogy, not story fact): Bai Yin is the archetype of a "firekeeper without a name" — every culture that has a perpetual hearth has the same figure; the novel gives the figure a name and a family history.

## 11 · Fire in philosophy — the ever-living first element

- **Heraclitus** (c. 535–475 BC) held "all things flow" and identified **fire** as the primal element from which everything arises: "This ordered cosmos, the same for all, no god or human made; instead it was, is, and will be an ever-living fire, kindling measure and being extinguished in measure." — the closest philosophical kin of the stove's seven hundred years.
- **Aristotle** cataloged fire among the four elements (earth, water, air, fire) as the element that produces both light and destruction — not as an agent of the gods but as the substrate of all thermal transformation.
- In the **Vedic Agnihotra**, fire is the most direct address to the divine — cows, ghee, butter, milk are offered into the sacred fire twice a day; the fire is not a god but the medium through which the offered reaches heaven.
- Crossrealm mapping (canon analogy, not story fact): Bai Yin's "watching the flame until it became the only thing I was sure of" and the ancestor-fire's "vigil of 600 years" are two instant stops on the Heraclitean line — "still the fire burns, still the cosmos persists" — the novel turns this into a story about who tends it.
