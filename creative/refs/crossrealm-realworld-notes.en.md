## 1 · Qiu Fen / the autumn equinox (24 solar terms · 二十四节气)

- The autumn equinox is one of the Twenty-Four Solar Terms in the Chinese lunisolar calendar:
  the Sun crosses the celestial equator southward and **day and night are of nearly equal length
  everywhere on Earth**; in the Northern Hemisphere it falls around 21–24 September every year
  (±1 day, depending on the year's cycle).
- The Twenty-Four Solar Terms are a 15°-longitude grid of the ecliptic built by
  agricultural civilization to time the seasons — Qiu Fen sits between Bai Lu (Cold Dew, 白露） and Han Lu （寒露）.
- Qiu Fen is mid-autumn: the harvest moon (中秋节） follows close behind — the year's brightest full moon,
  the year's coolest air, the year's deepest shadows. Moon-offering at mid-autumn is the rhyme: harvest → moon → home.
- The Old Saying "秋分昼夜一般长" (at Qiu Fen, day and night are equally long) remains common Chinese proverb today.
- Crossrealm mapping (canon analogy, not story fact): Volume 104 chose the equinox to light both fires together —
  the literal "day and night of equal length" became a mirrored pair of fire-keeping rituals across 700 years.
  The real world has no fire rite on Qiu Fen — this is a creative invention in the story.

## 2 · Betula platyphylla — white birch (白桦 · 樺 · Betula pendula)

- The genus Betula belongs to the family Betulaceae. White birch (Betula platyphylla) is native
  to the cool sub-boreal belt of the Northern Hemisphere — northeastern and north China, Inner Mongolia, Siberia,
  northern North America (Alaska), Scandinavia.
- The Chinese white-birch tree has a white, thin, papery bark that peels in horizontal sheets —
  the resinous part of the bark burns steadily and cleanly, making it the classic fuel for sustained fire
  across the Russian taiga and the Chinese northeast.
- Birch bark ("birch casing", 桦树皮） was widely used in pre-modern China and Russia for writing,
  roofing, torches (long-burning, bright and steady), canoe-building, and storage; Chinese records attest the use
  of birch bark at least as early as the Han dynasty.
- The Korean peninsula and northern China use white birch as the quintessential symbol of the student era and exam season.
- Russia's national tree is the birch; the Orthodox Russian Church blesses birch branches on Trinity Sunday today;
  Yesenin's poems on birch are ubiquitous in Russian schoolrooms.
- Crossrealm mapping (canon analogy, not story fact): the white birch that Bai Yin stored 600 years ago, the
  equinox where both fires burn the same birch — the rite is an invented mythology built on birch's real properties
  (resin = steady fire; white = winter = home). Not a historical practice.

## 3 · Zao Jun — the Kitchen God, and Chinese hearth culture (祭灶 · 灶神 · 火塘)

- The Chinese kitchen god, **Zao Jun （灶神）**, is arguably the most universal "household deity" in Chinese folk religion:
  he watches over the kitchen — the center of family life — records the deeds of the family, then on (or near)
  the 23rd or 24th day of the last lunar month ascends to report to Heaven.
- Families celebrate "Little New Year" (小年）: they offer Zao Jun sweets (sugar-candy to stick his mouth shut,
  or maltose to his lips), bamboo leaves, fish, pork — and burn paper "last supper" offerings (祭灶）.
- The sacrifice ritual appears at least by the Han dynasty; the Liji （礼记） describes 祀灶 — "to honor the stove":
  "one may not sacrifice to the stove for one's family, but it may well take care of the stove for all the people" —
  the stove is the home's sovereign of diet and of family life.
- Chinese southwest （丽江， 贵州， 云南）: the **火塘 (huǒtáng)** — the firepit at the center of the house —
  is literally the center of every household of the Naxi, Bai, Pumi and Miao-Yi peoples; house rules govern the hearth,
  and the first piece of wood of the year is "the life-fire" （命火） — the stove never goes out while the family endures.
  The phrase "火塘不灭，寨不散" ("while the firepit burns, the village endures") is today still printed in
  some Naxi villages for the New Year.
- Chinese southeast （绍兴， 苏州） — Zhentaobei （灶头边） household altars and 灶马 (Zao Ma, "stove horses" — the paper steeds on which Zao Jun ascends).
- Crossrealm mapping (canon analogy): Zao Jun → the guardian of the kitchen flame is a role literally
  designed for "白茵守灶". And the line in Wei Duxing （卫计局/守火） — **"灶火不灭，人就不散"** is a
  direct descendant of the historical Chinese hearth belief "火塘不灭，寨不散". The story did not borrow
  it whole — it made the keeper a woman, made the fire a person, removed the god who judges.

## 4 · Hestia · Vesta — the western hearth cult

- **Hestia** (Greek) is one of the twelve Olympians. She is the goddess of the hearth, of the domestic fire —
  the fire that was not optional, ever, in any Greek household; she is the still center that Athena, Zeus and Apollo bend around.
  Her symbol is a plain, unadorned flame — no monument, no weapon, no wrenched mythology. The Greeks called her
  "the common fire of all cities."
- **Vesta** (Roman) is her twin, center of the Forum's Temple of Vesta. **The Vestal Virgins** —
  six priestesses chosen in childhood — tended Vesta's sacred, never-dying flame inside the Forum.
  If the flame went out, the omens were catastrophic: Rome was believed to fall with it. The Vestal was virginal —
  so the fire's continuity was not family blood but a vow — and her 30-year tenure ran from age 10 to 40.
  If it finally extinguished — her failure, to be punished with entombment alive.
- Throughout the West today, the **eternal flame** is used at national tombs of the Unknown Soldier and at
  the tomb of JFK; the Holy Fire at Jerusalem's Church of the Holy Sepulchre at Pascha (Holy Saturday) is
  celebrated every year by millions — fire that no human hands visibly rekindle.
- The Olympic flame ceremony (Olympia, every Olympiad since 1928) rekindles the Olympic fire by a parabolic mirror at
  the site of Zeus's sanctuary — continuity of flame from ancient Greece is literally performed: same place, same light.
- Crossrealm mapping (canon analogy): Bai Yin ↔ a Vestal-like keeper; the "never-dying stove" ↔ Vesta's flame;
  the iron box and the ashes ↔ the sacred ash receptacles of the Vestal tempel. The novel replaced the sex-and-hierarchy
  requirement ("never a family lineage") with "fire passes down a family, but the family does not own the fire" — that, and only that, is the invention.

## 5 · Eternal flames — why humanity keeps the light alive

- The **Arch of Triumph** in Paris, dedicated to the Unknown Soldier of WWI, has burned continuously since 1923 — every evening at 6:30 p.m. the flame is rekindled by the last surviving member of the family.
- **JFK's tomb**, Arlington Cemetery, Washington D.C. — the eternal flame lit at his burial (1963) still burns today.
- **Hiroshima** — the peace flame, lit in 1964, will remain lit until the last nuclear weapon is destroyed.
- **Moscow** — the Eternal Flame at the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, Kremlin Wall, has never been extinguished since 1967; every Russian military school maintains one uninterrupted flame.
- The Tokyo War Shrine fire （靖国， burnt 1960–1985) and **Ise Grand Shrine's** Shikinen Sengu — the complete rebuilding of the Inner and Outer Shrines every 20 years since AD 690, 62 consecutive rebuildings so far — the newest becoming the oldest in the world.
- **Crossrealm mapping (canon analogy)**: the stove's seven hundred years are not the point; what the novel cares about — and what all human eternal flames actually are — is a chain of memory kept by hands, kept by people, kept alive not as a spectacle but as a household obligation. The Chinese hearth cult and the Vestals and these tombs are all the same instinct.

## 6 · Autumn festivals — harvest and shared light

- **Mid-Autumn Festival (八月十五， Zhōngqiūjié)**: lunar August 15, around the autumn equinox (the full moon nearest to it). Moon-offering, moon-cakes, reuniting with family; Chinese poems about the moon are essentially universal.
- **Chuseok** (Korea): the same astronomical moment; families visit the ancestral graves,
  eat songpyeon (rice cake), and celebrate the harvest — equivalent of Thanksgiving but centered on the ancestors.
- **Jūgoya (十五夜)** (Japan): "the fifteenth-night moon" — Tsukimi, moon-viewing; paper lanterns, dumpling offerings, no fire rites.
- **Lễ Trung Thu** (Vietnam): Trung Thu festival — moon-viewing, lion dance, ingot-shaped cakes.
- **Thanksgiving** (U.S.): fourth Thursday of November — different calendar but the same harvest logic.
- **Sukkot** (Judaism): 15th of Tishrei — booths and harvest, offers in the Temple.
- The Chinese equinox rite — 昼夜平分 — is the hinge point in festival logic: **"day and night the same length, harvest and duty the same measure"**. In the novel, the two fires are the two halves of the same day at the same moment — a religious structure the real world has but none of these festivals execute exactly.
- Crossrealm mapping (canon analogy): mid-autumn = fullness = reunion = the chance every year that the family briefs its promises; the novel took the logic of the moon and applied it to the fire — a full stove instead of a full moon, with the ancestor-flame as the other half of the same year. Fiction maps the calendar to the story; the real world does this in four dozen ways; the novel chose its own.
